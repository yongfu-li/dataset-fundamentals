    2.6 Probability Sampling Theory: Simple Random, Stratified & Cluster Derivations

    Collection instruments gather measurements, but sampling theory determines whose measurements enter the dataset and what statistical claims can be generalized to the broader population [Cochran1977, Lohr, 2021, Groves et al., 2009].

    In machine learning and data science, engineers frequently draw samples opportunistically (e.g., whichever records arrive first in a database query). However, non-probability draws systematically corrupt empirical risk estimates.

    To guarantee unbiased estimators, dataset collection must satisfy the formal requirements of Probability Sampling: every unit in the target population \(\mathcal{U}\) must have a strictly positive, mathematically calculable inclusion probability \(\pi_i = P(i \in \mathcal{S}) > 0\) [Neyman1934].

    1. Mathematical Foundations of Probability Sampling

    Let \(\mathcal{U} = \{1, 2, \dots, N\}\) denote a finite population of size \(N\). A sampling design assigns a probability \(P(\mathcal{S})\) to every possible subset \(\mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{U}\).

    The first-order inclusion probability for unit \(i\) is:

    \[ \pi_i = \sum_{\mathcal{S} \ni i} P(\mathcal{S}) \]

    Under the foundational Horvitz-Thompson Estimator [Horvitz1952], the unbiased estimator of the population total \(Y = \sum_{i=1}^N y_i\) is weighted inversely by inclusion probability:

    \[ \hat{Y}_{\text{HT}} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}} \frac{y_i}{\pi_i} \]

    If inclusion probabilities are unknown or zero for certain sub-populations (e.g., offline citizens), \(\hat{Y}_{\text{HT}}\) cannot be computed, and external statistical validity collapses.

    Industrial Best Practice: Neyman Optimal Allocation Under Strata Cost Constraints

    When stratification strata have unequal observation variances \(\sigma_h^2\) and heterogeneous sampling costs \(c_h\), always use Neyman-Tschuprow Optimal Allocation: $\(n_h = n \frac{N_h \sigma_h / \sqrt{c_h}}{\sum_k N_k \sigma_k / \sqrt{c_k}}\)$ This mathematically minimizes the global variance of the population estimator for a fixed sampling budget.

    Common Pitfall / Anti-Pattern: Conflating Cluster Sampling with Stratified Sampling

    Stratified sampling selects some elements from all strata (reducing variance by ensuring subgroup representation). Cluster sampling selects all elements from some clusters (reducing field collection cost, but increasing variance due to intra-cluster correlation \(\rho_{\text{ICC}}\)). Confusing the two leads to severe under-estimation of confidence intervals.

    2. Simple Random Sampling (SRS) & Sample Size Determination

    In Simple Random Sampling without replacement (\(\text{SRSWOR}\)), every subset of size \(n\) has equal probability of selection: \(P(\mathcal{S}) = \binom{N}{n}^{-1}\). Hence, \(\pi_i = \frac{n}{N}\) for all \(i\).

    A. Estimator Variance & Finite Population Correction (FPC)

    The sample mean estimator is \(\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\). Its variance is:

    \[ \text{Var}(\bar{y}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \cdot \left(\frac{N - n}{N - 1}\right) \approx \frac{s^2}{n} \cdot \left(1 - \frac{n}{N}\right) \]

    Where \(\left(1 - \frac{n}{N}\right)\) is the Finite Population Correction (FPC) factor. When the sampling fraction \(\frac{n}{N} < 0.05\), the FPC approaches \(1.0\) and can be omitted.

    B. Sample Size Determination (Cochran's Formula)

    To achieve a desired margin of error \(e\) at confidence level \(1 - \alpha\) (with critical value \(Z_{\alpha/2}\)):

    \[ n_0 = \frac{Z_{\alpha/2}^2 \cdot p(1 - p)}{e^2} \quad (\text{Categorical Proportion}) \]
    \[ n_0 = \frac{Z_{\alpha/2}^2 \cdot \sigma^2}{e^2} \quad (\text{Continuous Metric}) \]

    Adjusting for a finite population \(N\):

    \[ n = \frac{n_0}{1 + \frac{n_0 - 1}{N}} \]

    For a \(95\%\) confidence level (\(Z = 1.96\)) and \(e = 0.03\) margin of error on an unknown proportion (\(p = 0.5\) for maximum variance):

    \[ n_0 = \frac{(1.96)^2 \cdot (0.25)}{(0.03)^2} = \frac{0.9604}{0.0009} \approx 1{,}067 \text{ observations} \]

    3. Stratified Random Sampling & Neyman Optimal Allocation

    When a population is heterogeneous across subgroups but homogeneous within them, Stratified Sampling partitions \(\mathcal{U}\) into \(H\) disjoint strata \(\mathcal{U}_1, \dots, \mathcal{U}_H\) (\(\sum N_h = N\)), drawing independent random samples of size \(n_h\) (\(\sum n_h = n\)) from each stratum.

    The stratified mean estimator is:

    \[ \bar{y}_{\text{st}} = \sum_{h=1}^H W_h \bar{y}_h, \quad W_h = \frac{N_h}{N} \]
    \[ \text{Var}(\bar{y}_{\text{st}}) = \sum_{h=1}^H W_h^2 \cdot \frac{S_h^2}{n_h} \cdot \left(1 - \frac{n_h}{N_h}\right) \]

    Allocation Strategies: Proportional vs. Neyman

    1. Proportional Allocation: \(n_h = n \cdot W_h\). Samples each stratum strictly by its population share.
    2. Neyman Optimal Allocation: Minimizes sample variance \(\text{Var}(\bar{y}_{\text{st}})\) for a fixed total sample size \(n\) by allocating more samples to strata that are larger or have higher internal variance \(S_h\):
    \[ n_h = n \cdot \frac{N_h S_h}{\sum_{k=1}^H N_k S_k} \]

    [!TIP] Why Stratification Beats SRS: Stratified sampling never has a higher variance than simple random sampling (\(\text{Var}(\bar{y}_{\text{st}}) \le \text{Var}_{\text{srs}}(\bar{y})\)). The variance reduction is directly proportional to the between-strata differences in means.

    4. Cluster Sampling & The Design Effect (Deff)

    When individual population frames are unavailable or geographically scattered (e.g., inspecting hospital wards, schools, or smart meters), Cluster Sampling selects \(k\) clusters out of \(K\) total clusters and measures units within the chosen clusters.

    While operationally economical, cluster sampling suffers from positive intra-cluster correlation: units within the same cluster tend to resemble one another.

    The Design Effect (Deff) & Kish's Formulation

    The penalty for cluster sampling relative to SRS is quantified by the Design Effect (\(\text{Deff}\)) [Kish1965]:

    \[ \text{Deff} = \frac{\text{Var}_{\text{cluster}}(\bar{y})}{\text{Var}_{\text{srs}}(\bar{y})} = 1 + (m - 1) \cdot \rho \]

    Where: - \(m\) is the average cluster size. - \(\rho\) is the Intra-Class Correlation Coefficient (ICC):

    \[ \rho = \frac{\sigma_{\text{between}}^2}{\sigma_{\text{between}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{within}}^2} \]

    The Effective Sample Size (\(n_{\text{eff}}\)) is:

    \[ n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{n}{\text{Deff}} = \frac{n}{1 + (m - 1)\rho} \]

    If \(m = 100\) students per school and \(\rho = 0.10\), \(\text{Deff} = 1 + 99(0.10) = 10.9\). A sample of \(n = 1{,}000\) clustered students yields the statistical precision of only:

    \[ n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1{,}000}{10.9} \approx 92 \text{ independent observations!} \]

    💻 Production Implementation: Probability Sampling & Horvitz-Thompson Estimator

    Below is a production sampling engine providing Stratified Sampling with Neyman Allocation and the unbiased Horvitz-Thompson population total estimator.

    import numpy as np
from typing import Dict, List, Tuple

class ProbabilitySamplingEngine:
    """Implements probability sampling and Horvitz-Thompson estimation."""

    @staticmethod
    def neyman_allocation(strata_sizes: Dict[str, int], strata_stds: Dict[str, float], total_sample_size: int) -> Dict[str, int]:
        """Calculates optimal sample allocation per stratum minimizing estimator variance."""
        numerator = {k: strata_sizes[k] * strata_stds[k] for k in strata_sizes}
        total_weight = sum(numerator.values())
        return {k: max(1, int(np.round((numerator[k] / total_weight) * total_sample_size))) for k in strata_sizes}

    @staticmethod
    def horvitz_thompson_total(sample_values: List[float], inclusion_probabilities: List[float]) -> float:
        """Unbiased Horvitz-Thompson estimator of population total: Y_hat = sum(y_i / pi_i)."""
        return float(np.sum(np.array(sample_values) / np.array(inclusion_probabilities)))

def test_sampling_engine():
    sizes = {"Urban": 10000, "Suburban": 5000, "Rural": 2000}
    stds = {"Urban": 12.0, "Suburban": 8.0, "Rural": 25.0}
    sample_n = 500

    alloc = ProbabilitySamplingEngine.neyman_allocation(sizes, stds, sample_n)
    assert sum(alloc.values()) in [499, 500, 501]
    assert alloc["Rural"] > 80, "High variance stratum should receive boosted allocation"

    # Horvitz-Thompson test: 10 items sampled from 100 with uniform pi = 0.10
    sample = [5.0] * 10
    pis = [0.10] * 10
    estimated_total = ProbabilitySamplingEngine.horvitz_thompson_total(sample, pis)
    assert np.isclose(estimated_total, 500.0), f"Expected 500.0, got {estimated_total}"
    print("✓ Probability sampling & Horvitz-Thompson assertions passed!")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    test_sampling_engine()

    💡 Key Engineering Takeaways

    1. Probability Sampling Invariant: Unbiased empirical estimators require non-zero, known inclusion probabilities \(\pi_i > 0\) for all units.
    2. Neyman Allocation Efficiency: In stratified sampling, sample more heavily from strata with higher standard deviation \(S_h\) to minimize total estimation variance.
    3. The Cluster Inflation Penalty: High intra-class correlation (\(\rho > 0\)) causes cluster sampling to drastically inflate variance; always compute Kish's \(\text{Deff}\) to establish effective sample size.

    ❓ Interactive Self-Assessment Micro-Checks

    Self-Assessment: Quantifying the Design Effect (Deff) in Cluster Sampling

    A market research team samples 50 retail stores (clusters) and interviews 20 customers per store (\(m = 20\)). The intra-cluster correlation coefficient for purchasing habits is \(\rho = 0.15\). Calculate the Design Effect (\(Deff\)) and effective sample size (\(n_{\text{eff}}\)) if the total interviewed sample is \(n = 1,000\).

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Answer: The Design Effect (\(Deff\)) measures how much cluster sampling inflates the variance of the estimator compared to Simple Random Sampling (SRS): $\(Deff = 1 + (m - 1)\rho = 1 + (20 - 1) \times 0.15 = 1 + 19 \times 0.15 = 1 + 2.85 = 3.85\)$ The effective sample size is: $\(n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{n}{Deff} = \frac{1000}{3.85} \approx 260\)$ Despite interviewing 1,000 individuals, the sample carries only the statistical information equivalent to 260 independent observations because customers within the same store exhibit correlated behavior.

    Self-Assessment: Horvitz-Thompson Estimator and Unequal Selection Probabilities

    Under what mathematical condition is the Horvitz-Thompson estimator \(\hat{Y}_{HT} = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{y_i}{\pi_i}\) guaranteed to be strictly unbiased for total population value \(Y = \sum_{i=1}^N y_i\)?

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Answer: The Horvitz-Thompson estimator is strictly unbiased if and only if every element in the target population has a strictly positive probability of selection: $\(\forall i \in \{1, \dots, N\}, \quad \pi_i > 0\)$ If any population subset has \(\pi_i = 0\) (under-coverage), \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}_{HT}] \ne Y\), producing persistent, irrecoverable sampling bias.
