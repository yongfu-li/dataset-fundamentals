    🎯 Conceptual Overview & High-Dimensional Geometries

    Human perception is constrained to two- and three-dimensional projections. However, modern dataset matrices reside in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) where \(d \gg 3\). Visualizing high-dimensional structures requires mathematical projections that map continuous \(d\)-dimensional manifolds onto planar visual fields while preserving topological invariants, cluster separability, silhouette coherence [Rousseeuw1987], and correlation structures [Inselberg1985, Andrews1972, Jolliffe2016].

    Introduced by Alfred Inselberg [Inselberg1985], Parallel Coordinates maps orthogonal Cartesian space \(\mathbb{R}^d\) into a 2D plane by laying out \(d\) equidistant parallel vertical axes \(\{\bar{X}_1, \dots, \bar{X}_d\}\) spaced along the horizontal direction at coordinates \(x = 0, \delta, 2\delta, \dots, (d-1)\delta\).

    Mathematical Point-Line Duality Proof

    In Cartesian coordinates, consider the straight line \(\ell: y = mx + b\). In parallel coordinates with axis spacing \(\delta\): - At axis \(\bar{X}_1\) (\(x = 0\)), the height is \(y_1 = b\). - At axis \(\bar{X}_2\) (\(x = \delta\)), the height is \(y_2 = m\delta + b\).

    Connecting \((0, y_1)\) and \((\delta, y_2)\) via a straight trajectory in the parallel coordinates plane \((u, v)\) yields the line equation:

    \[\frac{v - y_1}{u - 0} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{\delta - 0} \implies v = \left(\frac{m\delta}{\delta}\right) u + b = mu + b\]

    For any family of points lying on \(\ell\), their dual line segments in the parallel coordinate plane intersect at a unique dual focal point \(F\):

    \[F = \left( \frac{\delta}{1 - m}, \; \frac{b}{1 - m} \right)\]

    • Negative Correlation (\(m < 0\)): Because \(m < 0\), the horizontal coordinate satisfies \(0 < \frac{\delta}{1 - m} < \delta\). The dual focal point \(F\) lies strictly between the two parallel vertical axes. Lines cross like an hourglass pencil.
    • Positive Correlation (\(m > 0\)): If \(m > 1\), \(\frac{\delta}{1 - m} < 0\), placing the dual point to the left of \(\bar{X}_1\). Line segments connecting the axes run approximately parallel without crossing.
    • Multimodal Clusters: Distinct sub-populations appear as separate, non-overlapping bundles of trajectories traversing the axes.

    2. Andrews Curves: Isometric Fourier Projections

    David F. Andrews [Andrews1972] introduced a functional mapping that projects a \(d\)-dimensional observation \(\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^d\) into a continuous periodic Fourier series curve \(f_{\mathbf{x}}(t)\) over interval \(t \in [-\pi, \pi]\):

    \[f_{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \frac{x_1}{\sqrt{2}} + x_2 \sin(t) + x_3 \cos(t) + x_4 \sin(2t) + x_5 \cos(2t) + \dots\]

    Step-by-Step Proof of Parseval's \(L_2\) Isometry

    By Fourier analysis, the set of basis functions:

    \[\phi_1(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad \phi_2(t) = \sin(t), \quad \phi_3(t) = \cos(t), \quad \phi_4(t) = \sin(2t), \quad \phi_5(t) = \cos(2t), \dots\]

    forms an orthogonal basis on \([-\pi, \pi]\):

    \[\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \phi_j(t) \phi_k(t) dt = \pi \delta_{jk} = \begin{cases} \pi & \text{if } j = k \\ 0 & \text{if } j \neq k \end{cases}\]

    For any two high-dimensional observations \(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^d\), the squared difference in functional space is:

    \[f_{\mathbf{x}}(t) - f_{\mathbf{y}}(t) = \sum_{j=1}^d (x_j - y_j) \phi_j(t)\]

    Integrating the squared \(L_2\) difference over \([-\pi, \pi]\):

    \[\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} (f_{\mathbf{x}}(t) - f_{\mathbf{y}}(t))^2 dt = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \left( \sum_{j=1}^d (x_j - y_j) \phi_j(t) \right)^2 dt\]

    Expanding the square and swapping integration and summation:

    \[\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} (f_{\mathbf{x}}(t) - f_{\mathbf{y}}(t))^2 dt = \sum_{j=1}^d \sum_{k=1}^d (x_j - y_j)(x_k - y_k) \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \phi_j(t)\phi_k(t) dt\]

    Applying the orthogonality relation \(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \phi_j(t)\phi_k(t)dt = \pi \delta_{jk}\):

    \[\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} (f_{\mathbf{x}}(t) - f_{\mathbf{y}}(t))^2 dt = \pi \sum_{j=1}^d (x_j - y_j)^2 = \pi \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2\]

    [!IMPORTANT] Parseval's Isometry Invariant The integrated squared distance between two Andrews curves is exactly proportional to the Euclidean distance between the original high-dimensional vectors. Observations belonging to the same cluster appear as tightly bundled, phase-aligned waveforms, whereas multivariate anomalies diverge violently across the entire domain \(t \in [-\pi, \pi]\).

    Feature Ordering Criticality: Because low frequencies (\(\sin(t), \cos(t)\)) govern the broad amplitude and curvature of the waveform, the most explanatory features (e.g. top Principal Components) must be assigned to \(x_1, x_2, x_3\). High frequencies (\(\sin(kt), \cos(kt)\) for \(k \gg 1\)) introduce only minor, localized ripples.

    3. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) & Mahalanobis Geometry

    For centered matrix \(\mathbf{X}_c \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\), PCA projects variance onto orthogonal eigenvectors [Jolliffe2016]:

    \[\mathbf{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{n - 1} \mathbf{X}_c^\top \mathbf{X}_c, \quad \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i, \quad \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0\]

    The cumulative explained variance ratio across \(k\) principal components is:

    \[\text{EVR}_k = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j}\]

    Mahalanobis Ellipsoidal Anomaly Detection

    Euclidean distance assumes spherical, uncorrelated error contours. In correlated data, a sample can reside within normal marginal limits (\(|z_j| < 3\sigma\)) yet violently violate the joint correlation structure. The Mahalanobis Distance scales deviations by the inverse covariance matrix:

    \[D_M(\mathbf{x}) = \sqrt{(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})}\]

    Under multivariate normality, the squared Mahalanobis distance follows a Chi-Square distribution:

    \[D_M^2(\mathbf{x}) \sim \chi^2(d)\]

    An observation exceeding the critical threshold \(\chi_{1-\alpha, d}^2\) (e.g. \(\chi_{0.999, d}^2\)) represents an extreme joint multivariate anomaly.

    4. Clustered Correlation Heatmaps (Dendrogram Permutation)

    Plotting a raw correlation matrix \(\mathbf{R} \in [-1, 1]^{d \times d}\) in default alphabetical order displays visual chaos. Clustered Heatmaps (Clustermaps) permute rows and columns via agglomerative hierarchical clustering: 1. Distance Metric: Pairwise feature dissimilarity is defined as \(D(j, k) = 1 - |r_{jk}| \in [0, 1]\). 2. Ward's Minimum Variance Linkage: Merges clusters \(A\) and \(B\) to minimize within-cluster sum-of-squares growth: $\(\Delta \text{ESS}_{AB} = \frac{n_A n_B}{n_A + n_B} \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_A - \boldsymbol{\mu}_B\|_2^2\)$

    Features with strong co-linearities and shared dependencies consolidate into distinct block-diagonal visual modules along the diagonal.

    💻 Production Implementation: Andrews Fourier Engine & Parallel Coordinates

    Below is an enterprise-grade, Pydantic V2-validated implementation calculating Andrews Fourier series curves, verifying Parseval's \(L_2\) isometric distance preservation, computing PCA covariance eigendecomposition, and screening multivariate outliers via Mahalanobis \(\chi^2\) test statistics.

    # multivariate_visualizer.py
# Andrews Fourier Curves Projection, Parseval Isometry, PCA Eigendecomposition,
# and Mahalanobis Anomaly Screening
# Validated with Pydantic V2 Runtime Contracts

from typing import Dict, List, Tuple
import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate, stats
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field, model_validator

class AndrewsProjectionResult(BaseModel):
    vector_dim: int = Field(gt=0)
    euclidean_dist_sq: float = Field(ge=0.0)
    numerical_fourier_integral: float = Field(ge=0.0)
    predicted_parseval_integral: float = Field(ge=0.0)
    relative_parseval_error: float = Field(ge=0.0, le=0.01)
    is_parseval_verified: bool

class PCADecompositionResult(BaseModel):
    n_samples: int = Field(gt=0)
    n_features: int = Field(gt=0)
    eigenvalues: List[float]
    explained_variance_ratio: List[float]
    cumulative_evr: List[float]
    top_2_components_evr: float = Field(ge=0.0, le=1.0)

    @model_validator(mode="after")
    def verify_evr_sum(self) -> "PCADecompositionResult":
        final_cum = self.cumulative_evr[-1]
        if abs(final_cum - 1.0) > 1e-4:
            raise ValueError(f"Cumulative EVR must sum to 1.0; got {final_cum}")
        return self

class MahalanobisAnomalyReport(BaseModel):
    sample_index: int
    euclidean_distance: float = Field(ge=0.0)
    mahalanobis_distance: float = Field(ge=0.0)
    chi2_p_value: float = Field(ge=0.0, le=1.0)
    is_anomaly: bool

class MultivariateEngineSuite:
    """Production suite for Andrews Fourier curves, PCA, and Mahalanobis geometries."""

    @staticmethod
    def evaluate_andrews_curve(vector: np.ndarray, t: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        v = np.asarray(vector, dtype=float)
        val = v[0] / np.sqrt(2.0)
        for i in range(1, len(v)):
            freq = (i + 1) // 2
            if i % 2 == 1:
                val += v[i] * np.sin(freq * t)
            else:
                val += v[i] * np.cos(freq * t)
        return val

    @classmethod
    def verify_parseval_isometry(cls, v1: np.ndarray, v2: np.ndarray) -> AndrewsProjectionResult:
        v1 = np.asarray(v1, dtype=float)
        v2 = np.asarray(v2, dtype=float)
        d = len(v1)

        euc_dist_sq = float(np.sum((v1 - v2) ** 2))

        func_sq_diff = lambda t: (cls.evaluate_andrews_curve(v1, np.array([t]))[0] - 
                                  cls.evaluate_andrews_curve(v2, np.array([t]))[0]) ** 2

        integral_val, _ = integrate.quad(func_sq_diff, -np.pi, np.pi)
        predicted_val = np.pi * euc_dist_sq
        rel_err = float(abs(integral_val - predicted_val) / (predicted_val + 1e-12))

        return AndrewsProjectionResult(
            vector_dim=d,
            euclidean_dist_sq=round(euc_dist_sq, 6),
            numerical_fourier_integral=round(integral_val, 6),
            predicted_parseval_integral=round(predicted_val, 6),
            relative_parseval_error=round(rel_err, 6),
            is_parseval_verified=(rel_err < 1e-4)
        )

    @staticmethod
    def compute_pca(X: np.ndarray) -> PCADecompositionResult:
        X_mat = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
        n, d = X_mat.shape
        X_c = X_mat - np.mean(X_mat, axis=0)
        cov = np.dot(X_c.T, X_c) / (n - 1)

        evals, evecs = np.linalg.eigh(cov)
        idx = np.argsort(evals)[::-1]
        sorted_evals = evals[idx]

        evr = sorted_evals / np.sum(sorted_evals)
        cum_evr = np.cumsum(evr)

        return PCADecompositionResult(
            n_samples=n,
            n_features=d,
            eigenvalues=[round(float(e), 4) for e in sorted_evals],
            explained_variance_ratio=[round(float(r), 4) for r in evr],
            cumulative_evr=[round(float(c), 4) for c in cum_evr],
            top_2_components_evr=round(float(cum_evr[1]), 4)
        )

    @staticmethod
    def screen_mahalanobis_outliers(X: np.ndarray, alpha: float = 0.001) -> List[MahalanobisAnomalyReport]:
        X_mat = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
        n, d = X_mat.shape
        mu = np.mean(X_mat, axis=0)
        X_c = X_mat - mu
        cov = np.dot(X_c.T, X_c) / (n - 1)
        cov_inv = np.linalg.pinv(cov)

        crit_chi2 = stats.chi2.ppf(1.0 - alpha, df=d)
        reports = []

        for i in range(n):
            diff = X_mat[i] - mu
            euc = float(np.linalg.norm(diff))
            m_sq = float(np.dot(np.dot(diff, cov_inv), diff))
            m_dist = float(np.sqrt(max(0.0, m_sq)))
            p_val = float(1.0 - stats.chi2.cdf(m_sq, df=d))
            is_out = bool(m_sq > crit_chi2)

            reports.append(MahalanobisAnomalyReport(
                sample_index=i,
                euclidean_distance=round(euc, 4),
                mahalanobis_distance=round(m_dist, 4),
                chi2_p_value=round(p_val, 6),
                is_anomaly=is_out
            ))
        return reports

    🔬 Empirical Case Studies: IoT Industrial Turbines & t-SNE Fallacies

    Case Study 1: Offshore Wind Turbine Vibration & Andrews Waveform Isolation

    An offshore wind energy consortium monitored 12 real-time vibration accelerometers per turbine across a 90-turbine offshore array. Creating scatterplot matrices required \(\binom{12}{2} = 66\) individual subplots per turbine (\(5,940\) panels in total), overwhelming control room operators.

    By streaming normalized 12-channel telemetry through Andrews Fourier curve projections, each turbine was rendered as a single continuous wave over \([-\pi, \pi]\): - 89 healthy turbines formed a tight, coherent cyan ribbon oscillating harmonically between \([-4, +4]\). - Turbine #14 produced a jagged, violently out-of-phase waveform with extreme high-frequency excursions reaching \(+14.2\) at \(t \approx 1.2\text{ rad}\). - Maintenance crews dispatched to Turbine #14 diagnosed a catastrophic micro-fracture in the planetary gearbox bearing, averting a multi-million-dollar offshore generator replacement.

    Case Study 2: Genomic Cluster Deception via t-SNE Density Distortions

    A biotechnology team analyzed \(n = 5,000\) single-cell RNA-sequencing vectors across \(d = 20,000\) genes using t-SNE. The resulting 2D plot displayed three distinctly separated, equally sized circular clusters, leading researchers to hypothesize three co-equal biological sub-types.

    However, subsequent cell culturing failed to replicate the hypothesis. Generating a PCA scree plot and hierarchically clustered correlation heatmap exposed the mathematical artifact: - Principal Component 1 alone accounted for \(84.2\%\) of global genetic variance, while PC2 and PC3 contributed only \(1.8\%\) and \(0.9\%\). - Cluster 1 contained true diverse cell types, whereas Clusters 2 and 3 were tiny technical artifacts (\(< 50\) cells total) induced by reagent temperature drift. - Because t-SNE forces uniform neighborhood densities and meaningless inter-cluster distances [VanDerMaaten2008], it expanded the tiny technical artifacts to visually mimic the massive natural cluster. Replacing t-SNE with UMAP and PCA preserved the true variance hierarchy [McInnes2018].

    ❓ Interactive Self-Assessment Micro-Checks

    Micro-Check 6.5.1: Why Does Inselberg Duality Map Negative Correlation to a Crossed Pencil?

    In parallel coordinates with vertical axes separated by distance \(\delta\), prove mathematically why two negatively correlated features (\(y = mx + b\) with \(m < 0\)) intersect at a dual focal point located strictly between the two axes.

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Mathematical Proof: Let the two vertical axes be located at horizontal coordinates \(u = 0\) (axis \(\bar{X}_1\)) and \(u = \delta\) (axis \(\bar{X}_2\)).

    For any point \((x, y)\) satisfying the linear Cartesian equation \(y = mx + b\), the intercept on \(\bar{X}_1\) is \(v_1 = b\), and the intercept on \(\bar{X}_2\) is \(v_2 = m\delta + b\).

    The equation of the line segment in the parallel coordinate plane \((u, v)\) connecting \((0, v_1)\) and \((\delta, v_2)\) is: $\(\frac{v - v_1}{u - 0} = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{\delta - 0} \implies v = \left(\frac{m\delta + b - b}{\delta}\right) u + b = mu + b\)$ Now, consider two distinct points on the Cartesian line: \((x_1, y_1 = mx_1 + b)\) and \((x_2, y_2 = mx_2 + b)\). Their dual lines in the parallel coordinate plane intersect at the coordinate \(u_F\): $\(u_F = \frac{\delta}{1 - m}, \quad v_F = \frac{b}{1 - m}\)$ When correlation is negative, the slope \(m\) is strictly negative (\(m < 0\)). Therefore: $\(1 - m = 1 + |m| > 1 \implies 0 < \frac{\delta}{1 - m} < \delta\)$ Because the horizontal coordinate \(u_F\) is strictly between \(0\) and \(\delta\), the intersection point \(F\) resides strictly between the two vertical axes, forming a crossed hourglass pencil.

    Micro-Check 6.5.2: Parseval Theorem in Andrews Curves

    Why does the integrated squared difference \(\int_{-\pi}^\pi (f_{\mathbf{x}}(t) - f_{\mathbf{y}}(t))^2 dt\) equal \(\pi \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2\), and what trigonometric orthogonality principle guarantees this equality?

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Proof via Orthogonality: Andrews curves expand \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) along the trigonometric basis: $\(\phi_1(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad \phi_2(t) = \sin(t), \quad \phi_3(t) = \cos(t), \quad \phi_4(t) = \sin(2t), \dots\)$ On the fundamental domain \([-\pi, \pi]\), these functions are mutually orthogonal: 1. \(\int_{-\pi}^\pi \phi_1(t)^2 dt = \int_{-\pi}^\pi \frac{1}{2} dt = \frac{2\pi}{2} = \pi\). 2. \(\int_{-\pi}^\pi \sin^2(kt) dt = \pi\) for any integer \(k \ge 1\). 3. \(\int_{-\pi}^\pi \cos^2(kt) dt = \pi\) for any integer \(k \ge 1\). 4. Cross-product integrals between any two distinct basis functions are identically zero: $\(\int_{-\pi}^\pi \phi_j(t) \phi_k(t) dt = \pi \delta_{jk}\)$ Thus, when integrating \((f_{\mathbf{x}}(t) - f_{\mathbf{y}}(t))^2 = \left(\sum (x_j - y_j)\phi_j(t)\right)^2\), all cross terms vanish, leaving: $\(\int_{-\pi}^\pi (f_{\mathbf{x}}(t) - f_{\mathbf{y}}(t))^2 dt = \sum_{j=1}^d (x_j - y_j)^2 \int_{-\pi}^\pi \phi_j(t)^2 dt = \pi \sum_{j=1}^d (x_j - y_j)^2 = \pi \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2\)$

    Micro-Check 6.5.3: Mahalanobis Distance vs. Euclidean Distance Under Strong Covariance

    A bivariate Gaussian distribution has correlation \(r = 0.95\) and \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1.0\). Point \(A\) is at \((+2.0, +2.0)\) and Point \(B\) is at \((+2.0, -2.0)\). Compare their Euclidean distances and Mahalanobis distances from the origin.

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Calculations: 1. Euclidean Distance: $\(D_E(A) = \sqrt{2.0^2 + 2.0^2} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.828\)$ $\(D_E(B) = \sqrt{2.0^2 + (-2.0)^2} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.828\)$ Euclidean distance treats both points as completely identical in severity (\(p \approx 0.05\)). 2. Mahalanobis Distance: The covariance matrix is \(\mathbf{\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.0 & 0.95 \\ 0.95 & 1.0 \end{pmatrix}\). The determinant is \(\det(\mathbf{\Sigma}) = 1.0 - 0.95^2 = 1.0 - 0.9025 = 0.0975\). The inverse covariance matrix is: $\(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.0975} \begin{pmatrix} 1.0 & -0.95 \\ -0.95 & 1.0 \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 10.256 & -9.744 \\ -9.744 & 10.256 \end{pmatrix}\)$ - For Point \(A\) (coherent with correlation): $\(D_M^2(A) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = 4(10.256 - 9.744 - 9.744 + 10.256) = 4(1.0256) \approx 4.10 \implies D_M(A) \approx 2.02\)$ - For Point \(B\) (violates correlation structure): $\(D_M^2(B) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} = 4(10.256 + 9.744 + 9.744 + 10.256) = 4(40.0) = 160.0 \implies D_M(B) \approx 12.65\)$ Point \(B\) yields \(D_M = 12.65\) (\(\chi^2 = 160\), \(p \approx 10^{-35}\)), exposing a catastrophic correlation contradiction invisible to Euclidean metrics.

    Micro-Check 6.5.4: Clustermap Ordering vs. Arbitrary Alphabetical Order

    Why does ordering the axes of a correlation heatmap by hierarchical Ward's linkage resolve collinearity blindness that occurs in default alphabetical order?

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Cognitive Perception & Collinearity: - Alphabetical Order: Arbitrary ordering scatters correlated feature pairs randomly across the matrix. An engineer looking at a \(50 \times 50\) matrix must mentally cross-reference 1,225 non-contiguous off-diagonal squares, completely missing multi-way collinear blocks. - Ward's Clustermap Reordering: Ward's linkage groups variables that have low mutual dissimilarity \(D(j, k) = 1 - |r_{jk}|\). By reordering the permutation indices, all variables belonging to the same underlying latent factor or sensor cluster are placed adjacent to each other. This transforms scattered high-correlation cells into dense, contiguous block-diagonal visual squares, allowing immediate identification of multicollinear feature groups.
