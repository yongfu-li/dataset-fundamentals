    🎯 Conceptual Overview & Spectral Foundations

    When tabular data dimension \(d\) exceeds dozens or hundreds of features, pairwise bivariate projections collapse higher-order structural relationships into uninterpretable noise. Modern exploratory data analysis overcomes this curse of dimensionality through two complementary mathematical paradigms [Jolliffe2016, VanDerMaaten2008, McInnes2018, Carlsson2009]:

    1. Global Spectral Decomposition: Orthogonal linear projections (Principal Component Analysis) that maximize variance capture across the global coordinate space and preserve global Euclidean geometries.
    2. Local Manifold Embedding: Non-linear stochastic and topological mappings (t-SNE, UMAP, Persistent Homology) that model the underlying data-generating manifold, preserving local neighborhood affinities and revealing cluster topologies invisible to linear projections.

    📐 Mathematical Foundations & Formal Proofs

    1. Principal Component Analysis: Optimal Low-Rank Reconstruction

    Let centered data matrix \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) satisfy \(\frac{1}{n} \mathbf{1}_n^\top \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{0}^\top\). The sample covariance matrix is:

    \[\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n - 1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\]

    Rayleigh Quotient & Spectral Theorem Formulation

    The first principal component loading vector \(\mathbf{v}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^d\) seeks a unit-norm projection direction maximizing sample variance:

    \[\max_{\mathbf{v}_1} \mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}_1 \quad \text{subject to } \mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{v}_1 = 1\]

    Formulating the Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v}_1, \lambda_1) = \mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}_1 - \lambda_1 (\mathbf{v}_1^\top \mathbf{v}_1 - 1)\), setting the gradient to zero yields:

    \[\nabla_{\mathbf{v}_1} \mathcal{L} = 2 \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}_1 - 2 \lambda_1 \mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}_1 = \lambda_1 \mathbf{v}_1\]

    By the Spectral Theorem for symmetric positive semi-definite matrices, \(\mathbf{S}\) admits an orthogonal eigendecomposition:

    \[\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{V} \boldsymbol{\Lambda} \mathbf{V}^\top = \sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j \mathbf{v}_j \mathbf{v}_j^\top\]

    where eigenvalues are ordered \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0\), and \(\mathbf{V} = [\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_d]\) forms an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^d\).

    The Eckart-Young-Mirsky Theorem Proof

    Consider the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\):

    \[\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U} \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^\top\]

    where \(r = \min(n, d)\), \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r \ge 0\), and \(\lambda_j = \frac{\sigma_j^2}{n - 1}\).

    Theorem (Eckart-Young-Mirsky, 1936): For any integer \(k < r\), the truncated rank-\(k\) matrix \(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^\top\) is the unique global minimizer of the Frobenius reconstruction error among all rank-\(k\) matrices \(\mathbf{M} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\):

    \[\min_{\text{rank}(\mathbf{M}) \le k} \|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{M}\|_F^2 = \|\mathbf{X} - \hat{\mathbf{X}}_k\|_F^2 = \sum_{j=k+1}^r \sigma_j^2 = (n - 1) \sum_{j=k+1}^d \lambda_j\]

    Proof Induction: Let \(\mathbf{M}\) be any matrix of rank \(k\). By the rank-nullity theorem, the null space \(\ker(\mathbf{M})\) has dimension at least \(d - k\). Let \(\mathcal{V}_{k+1} = \text{span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_{k+1})\). Because \(\dim(\ker(\mathbf{M})) + \dim(\mathcal{V}_{k+1}) \ge (d - k) + (k + 1) = d + 1 > d\), there exists a non-zero unit vector \(\mathbf{z} \in \ker(\mathbf{M}) \cap \mathcal{V}_{k+1}\).

    Because \(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0}\) and \(\mathbf{z} = \sum_{j=1}^{k+1} c_j \mathbf{v}_j\) with \(\sum c_j^2 = 1\):

    \[\|(\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{M})\mathbf{z}\|_2^2 = \|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{z}\|_2^2 = \left\| \sum_{j=1}^{k+1} \sigma_j c_j \mathbf{u}_j \right\|_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{k+1} \sigma_j^2 c_j^2 \ge \sigma_{k+1}^2 \sum_{j=1}^{k+1} c_j^2 = \sigma_{k+1}^2\]

    Therefore, in the spectral operator norm \(\|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{M}\|_2 \ge \sigma_{k+1} = \|\mathbf{X} - \hat{\mathbf{X}}_k\|_2\). Extending to the unitarily invariant Frobenius norm \(\|\mathbf{A}\|_F^2 = \text{Tr}(\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{A})\) proves that \(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_k\) discards exactly the smallest \(d - k\) singular values, achieving the theoretical lower bound on reconstruction error \(\blacksquare\).

    Explained Variance Ratio & The Kaiser-Guttman Rule

    The Explained Variance Ratio (EVR) of component \(j\) is:

    \[\text{EVR}_j = \frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_{m=1}^d \lambda_m} = \frac{\lambda_j}{\text{Tr}(\mathbf{S})}\]

    For standardized data (\(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{R}\) where diagonal elements \(r_{jj} = 1\) and \(\text{Tr}(\mathbf{R}) = d\)), the mean eigenvalue is identically:

    \[\bar{\lambda} = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j = \frac{d}{d} = 1.0\]

    The Kaiser-Guttman Rule establishes that any principal component with \(\lambda_j < 1.0\) explains less variance than a single standardized raw feature, serving as a rigorous lower bound for component pruning.

    PCA Biplot Loading Geometry

    A biplot superimposes 2D observation scores \(\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{X} \mathbf{V}_{1:2} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 2}\) and variable loading vectors \(\mathbf{w}_j = \sqrt{\lambda_{1:2}} \odot \mathbf{v}_{j, 1:2} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) on a shared coordinate frame:

    \[\cos(\theta_{jk}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}_j^\top \mathbf{w}_k}{\|\mathbf{w}_j\| \|\mathbf{w}_k\|} \approx r_{jk}\]
    • \(\theta_{jk} \approx 0^\circ \implies \cos(\theta) \approx +1\): Strong positive correlation between feature \(j\) and feature \(k\).
    • \(\theta_{jk} \approx 90^\circ \implies \cos(\theta) \approx 0\): Orthogonality; uncorrelated features.
    • \(\theta_{jk} \approx 180^\circ \implies \cos(\theta) \approx -1\): Strong negative correlation.
    • Ray length \(\|\mathbf{w}_j\|_2 \in [0, 1]\) represents the proportion of variance in feature \(j\) accounted for by the first two principal axes (the quality of representation).

    2. t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE)

    Linear PCA forces global variance preservation, causing distinct non-linear clusters residing on sub-manifolds to overlap and collapse. t-SNE reformulates dimensionality reduction as a probability distribution matching problem [VanDerMaaten2008].

    High-Dimensional Gaussian Affinities

    In high-dimensional space \(\mathbb{R}^d\), the conditional probability that point \(\mathbf{x}_i\) picks \(\mathbf{x}_j\) as its neighbor is:

    \[p_{j \mid i} = \frac{\exp(-\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(-\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_k\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}, \quad p_{i \mid i} = 0\]

    Bandwidth \(\sigma_i\) is calibrated via binary root-finding so the Shannon entropy matches the target Perplexity:

    \[\text{Perp}(P_i) = 2^{H(P_i)} = 2^{-\sum_{j \neq i} p_{j \mid i} \log_2 p_{j \mid i}}\]

    To prevent outlier isolation, probabilities are symmetrized across \(n\) samples:

    \[p_{ij} = \frac{p_{j \mid i} + p_{i \mid j}}{2n}, \quad \sum_{i, j} p_{ij} = 1\]

    The Crowding Problem & Student-\(t\) Heavy Tails

    In dimension \(d\), the volume of a sphere of radius \(r\) scales exponentially:

    \[V_d(r) = \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2 + 1)} r^d \implies \frac{V_d(r)}{V_2(r)} \propto r^{d - 2}\]

    In high-dimensional space, the vast majority of volume is concentrated near the shell. When mapping hundreds of equidistant moderately separated points to \(\mathbb{R}^2\), the available 2D area scales only as \(r^2\). If target probabilities \(q_{ij}\) used a Gaussian kernel, moderate distances would exert immense repulsive force, crowding disparate clusters into the origin and crushing local cluster structures [VanDerMaaten2008, Wattenberg2016].

    t-SNE eliminates the crowding problem by substituting a Student-\(t\) distribution (Cauchy kernel, 1 degree of freedom) in low-dimensional space \(\mathbf{y}_i, \mathbf{y}_j \in \mathbb{R}^2\):

    \[q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \|\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j\|^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k} \sum_{l \neq k} (1 + \|\mathbf{y}_k - \mathbf{y}_l\|^2)^{-1}}, \quad q_{ii} = 0\]

    Because \((1 + d^2)^{-1}\) decays as an inverse-square power law (\(d^{-2}\)) rather than exponentially (\(\exp(-d^2)\)), moderately separated points in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) can be placed much further apart in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) without incurring severe probability penalties.

    KL Divergence Objective & Analytic Gradient Derivation

    The target embedding coordinates \(\mathbf{y}_i\) are found by minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence:

    \[\mathcal{L}_{\text{t-SNE}} = \text{KL}(P \parallel Q) = \sum_{i \neq j} p_{ij} \log \frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}\]

    Differentiating with respect to embedding coordinate \(\mathbf{y}_i\):

    \[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{t-SNE}}}{\partial \mathbf{y}_i} = 4 \sum_{j} (p_{ij} - q_{ij}) (\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j) (1 + \|\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j\|^2)^{-1}\]

    This gradient acts as a system of physical springs connecting point \(i\) to all other points \(j\): - Attractive Force (\(+4 p_{ij} q_{ij}^{-1} \dots\)): Pulls genuine high-dimensional neighbors together (\(p_{ij} \gg q_{ij}\)). - Repulsive Force (\(-4 q_{ij} \dots\)): Uniformly pushes all points apart, preventing collapse and clearing empty margins between clusters.

    [!WARNING] Critical EDA Caveat: t-SNE Distances Between Clusters Are Meaningless Because \(p_{ij} \approx 0\) for non-neighboring clusters, the attractive force between separate clusters is strictly zero. The relative distance between distant clusters in a t-SNE plot is an artifact of the optimization seed and the repulsive equilibrium, not an indicator of true high-dimensional Euclidean separation [Wattenberg2016].

    3. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)

    While t-SNE is exceptional for local clustering, it discards global manifold geometry. UMAP (McInnes et al., 2018) constructs a topological model grounded in Riemannian geometry and fuzzy simplicial sets [McInnes2018].

    Local Metric Space & Geodesic Affinity

    UMAP assumes data lies on a connected Riemannian manifold with a locally constant metric. For each point \(\mathbf{x}_i\), let \(\rho_i\) denote the distance to its nearest neighbor:

    \[\rho_i = \min \{ d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) \mid j \neq i \}\]

    This guarantees local connectivity. The directed fuzzy affinity from \(\mathbf{x}_i\) to \(\mathbf{x}_j\) is defined by:

    \[p_{j \mid i} = \exp\left( - \frac{\max(0, d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) - \rho_i)}{\sigma_i} \right)\]

    where \(\sigma_i\) solves \(\sum_{j \in k\text{-NN}} p_{j \mid i} = \log_2(k)\). Symmetrization uses the algebraic fuzzy union \(A \cup B = A + B - A \cap B\):

    \[\mu_{ij} = p_{j \mid i} + p_{i \mid j} - p_{j \mid i} p_{i \mid j}\]

    Fuzzy Simplicial Set Cross-Entropy Loss

    In low-dimensional space \(\mathbb{R}^2\), target affinities are modeled with parameters \(a, b\):

    \[\nu_{ij} = \left( 1 + a \|\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j\|^{2b} \right)^{-1}\]

    (Typically \(a \approx 1.577, b \approx 0.895\) for default min_dist = 0.1). UMAP minimizes the fuzzy set cross-entropy:

    \[\mathcal{L}_{\text{UMAP}} = \sum_{i \neq j} \left[ \underbrace{\mu_{ij} \log \frac{\mu_{ij}}{\nu_{ij}}}_{\text{Attractive Component}} + \underbrace{(1 - \mu_{ij}) \log \frac{1 - \mu_{ij}}{1 - \nu_{ij}}}_{\text{Global Repulsive Component}} \right]\]

    The crucial mathematical advantage of UMAP over t-SNE lies in the second term: when \(\mu_{ij} = 0\) (points are not neighbors), \((1 - \mu_{ij}) = 1\), creating an active repulsive potential \(\log(1 - \nu_{ij})^{-1}\) across all non-neighboring pairs. This continuous global repulsion preserves macro-scale inter-cluster distances and global topological trajectories far better than t-SNE.

    4. Topological Data Analysis (TDA) & Persistent Homology

    When manifolds contain non-Euclidean loops, voids, or continuous bifurcations (e.g., cellular cycle kinetics, financial regime switches), coordinate projections flatten the topology. Persistent Homology quantifies shape invariants that are coordinate-free and scale-invariant [Carlsson2009].

    The Vietoris-Rips Simplicial Complex

    Given point cloud \(X = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) and proximity threshold \(\epsilon \ge 0\), the Vietoris-Rips complex \(\text{VR}_\epsilon(X)\) is the simplicial complex containing simplex \(\sigma = [\mathbf{x}_{i_0}, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{i_k}]\) if and only if all pairwise distances satisfy:

    \[d(\mathbf{x}_{i_a}, \mathbf{x}_{i_b}) \le \epsilon \quad \forall 0 \le a < b \le k\]

    As \(\epsilon\) sweeps from \(0\) to \(\infty\), we obtain a nested sequence of topological spaces (a filtration):

    \[\emptyset = \text{VR}_0(X) \subseteq \text{VR}_{\epsilon_1}(X) \subseteq \dots \subseteq \text{VR}_{\epsilon_{\max}}(X)\]

    Betti Numbers & Persistence Lifespans

    The topology at scale \(\epsilon\) is characterized by Betti numbers \(\beta_k\): - \(\beta_0\): Number of connected components. - \(\beta_1\): Number of 1-dimensional cycles / tunnels (holes). - \(\beta_2\): Number of 2-dimensional enclosed cavities / voids.

    Each topological feature \(k\) has a birth threshold \(b_k\) and a death threshold \(d_k\). The persistence lifespan is:

    \[\ell_k = d_k - b_k\]
    • Topological Signal: Features with long lifespans \(\ell_k \gg 0\) persist across many geometric scales, representing genuine structural manifold loops.
    • Geometric Noise: Features with short lifespans \(\ell_k \approx 0\) born and killed rapidly represent random point sampling jitter.

    5. Quantitative Manifold Evaluation Metrics

    Visual embeddings can produce misleading clustering artifacts. Production EDA pipelines validate projections using mathematical metrics [Rousseeuw1987, Venna2001]:

    Trustworthiness & Continuity

    Given high-dimensional ranks \(r(i, j)\) and low-dimensional ranks \(\hat{r}(i, j)\):

    \[\text{Trustworthiness}(k) = 1 - \frac{2}{n k (2n - 3k - 1)} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j \in \hat{\mathcal{N}}_i^{(k)} \setminus \mathcal{N}_i^{(k)}} (r(i, j) - k)\]
    \[\text{Continuity}(k) = 1 - \frac{2}{n k (2n - 3k - 1)} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i^{(k)} \setminus \hat{\mathcal{N}}_i^{(k)}} (\hat{r}(i, j) - k)\]
    • Trustworthiness \(\in [0, 1]\): Penalizes artificial clusters (points placed close together in 2D that were not neighbors in high-D). High trustworthiness means what you see in 2D is genuine.
    • Continuity \(\in [0, 1]\): Penalizes manifold tearing (points that were neighbors in high-D that were torn apart in 2D).

import numpy as np

class PCAPerformanceSummary(BaseModel):
    n_samples: int = Field(gt=0, description="Total sample observations")
    n_features: int = Field(gt=0, description="Original feature dimensionality")
    eigenvalues: List[float] = Field(min_length=1, description="SVD covariance eigenvalues sorted descending")
    explained_variance_ratio: List[float] = Field(min_length=1, description="EVR fraction per component")
    cumulative_evr: List[float] = Field(min_length=1, description="Cumulative variance explained")
    kaiser_retained_count: int = Field(ge=1, description="Components exceeding average eigenvalue threshold")
    eckart_young_frobenius_error: float = Field(ge=0.0, description="Empirical rank-k reconstruction error")
    biplot_angle_cosine: float = Field(ge=-1.0, le=1.0, description="cos(theta) between loading vectors")
    empirical_pearson_r: float = Field(ge=-1.0, le=1.0, description="Ground truth sample correlation")
    biplot_discrepancy: float = Field(ge=0.0, description="Discrepancy |r - cos(theta)|")

    @field_validator("cumulative_evr")
    @classmethod
    def validate_cumulative_evr(cls, v: List[float]) -> List[float]:
        if not np.isclose(v[-1], 1.0, atol=1e-4):
            raise ValueError(f"Cumulative EVR must terminate at 1.0, got {v[-1]}")
        return v

class PCASpectralEngine:
    """
    SVD Spectral Decomposition and Biplot Projection Engine.
    Implements Eckart-Young reconstruction and biplot loading angle verifications.
    """
    def __init__(self, X: np.ndarray):
        self.X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
        self.n, self.d = self.X.shape
        self.mean_vec = np.mean(self.X, axis=0)
        self.X_c = self.X - self.mean_vec

    def decompose(self, k_eval: int = 2) -> Dict[str, Any]:
        # SVD: X_c = U * S * V^T
        U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(self.X_c, full_matrices=False)
        eigenvalues = (S ** 2) / (self.n - 1)
        total_var = np.sum(eigenvalues)
        evr = eigenvalues / total_var
        cum_evr = np.cumsum(evr)

        # Kaiser-Guttman count (eigenvalues above average eigenvalue)
        avg_eigenval = np.mean(eigenvalues)
        kaiser_count = int(np.sum(eigenvalues > avg_eigenval))

        # Eckart-Young rank-k low-rank reconstruction: X_hat_k = U_k * S_k * Vt_k
        U_k = U[:, :k_eval]
        S_k = np.diag(S[:k_eval])
        Vt_k = Vt[:k_eval, :]
        X_hat_k = U_k @ S_k @ Vt_k

        # Empirical Frobenius error vs. theoretical lower bound sum_{j>k} sigma_j^2
        frobenius_error = float(np.linalg.norm(self.X_c - X_hat_k, ord="fro"))
        theoretical_frobenius_error = float(np.sqrt(np.sum(S[k_eval:] ** 2)))

        scores = self.X_c @ Vt.T
        loadings = Vt.T  # Columns are eigenvectors

        return {
            "eigenvalues": [float(x) for x in eigenvalues],
            "evr": [float(x) for x in evr],
            "cumulative_evr": [float(x) for x in cum_evr],
            "kaiser_count": max(1, kaiser_count),
            "frobenius_error": frobenius_error,
            "theoretical_frobenius_error": theoretical_frobenius_error,
            "loadings": loadings,
            "scores": scores,
            "singular_values": S,
        }

    def verify_biplot_geometry(self, idx1: int, idx2: int) -> Tuple[float, float, float]:
        r_emp, _ = stats.pearsonr(self.X[:, idx1], self.X[:, idx2])
        dec = self.decompose()
        # 2D projection loadings scaled by singular values
        v1 = dec["loadings"][idx1, :2]
        v2 = dec["loadings"][idx2, :2]
        n1 = np.linalg.norm(v1)
        n2 = np.linalg.norm(v2)
        if n1 == 0 or n2 == 0:
            return float(r_emp), 0.0, 1.0
        cos_theta = float(np.dot(v1, v2) / (n1 * n2))
        return float(r_emp), cos_theta, float(abs(r_emp - cos_theta))


def compute_trustworthiness(X_high: np.ndarray, Y_low: np.ndarray, k: int = 5) -> float:
    """
    Computes Scikit-Learn style Trustworthiness metric:
    T(k) = 1 - (2 / (n * k * (2n - 3k - 1))) * sum_{i=1}^n sum_{j in U_i^(k)} (r(i, j) - k)
    """
    n = X_high.shape[0]
    if k >= n / 2:
        k = max(1, int(n / 4))

    D_high = np.sum((X_high[:, None, :] - X_high[None, :, :]) ** 2, axis=-1)
    np.fill_diagonal(D_high, np.inf)
    high_ranks = np.argsort(np.argsort(D_high, axis=1), axis=1) + 1

    D_low = np.sum((Y_low[:, None, :] - Y_low[None, :, :]) ** 2, axis=-1)
    np.fill_diagonal(D_low, np.inf)
    low_knn = np.argsort(D_low, axis=1)[:, :k]

    high_knn_mask = high_ranks <= k
    rank_sum = 0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in low_knn[i]:
            if not high_knn_mask[i, j]:
                rank_sum += (high_ranks[i, j] - k)

    denom = n * k * (2 * n - 3 * k - 1)
    t_val = 1.0 - (2.0 / denom) * rank_sum
    return float(np.clip(t_val, 0.0, 1.0))


def run_tsne_gradient_step(X: np.ndarray, perplexity: float = 20.0, steps: int = 30) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, float]:
    """
    Simulates t-SNE optimization steps on high-dimensional data using Student-t Cauchy kernel.
    """
    n = X.shape[0]
    D2 = np.sum((X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :]) ** 2, axis=-1)
    np.fill_diagonal(D2, 0.0)

    # Adaptive bandwidth sigma
    k_nn = int(np.clip(perplexity, 2, n - 1))
    sorted_d2 = np.sort(D2, axis=1)
    sigma2 = np.mean(sorted_d2[:, k_nn]) + 1e-6
    P = np.exp(-D2 / (2 * sigma2))
    np.fill_diagonal(P, 0.0)
    P = (P + P.T) / 2.0
    P = P / np.sum(P)
    P = np.maximum(P, 1e-15)
    P = P / np.sum(P)

    # PCA initialization
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X - np.mean(X, axis=0), full_matrices=False)
    Y = U[:, :2] * S[:2] * 0.1
    lr = 50.0

    for _ in range(steps):
        diff = Y[:, None, :] - Y[None, :, :]
        dist2 = np.sum(diff ** 2, axis=-1)
        num = 1.0 / (1.0 + dist2)
        np.fill_diagonal(num, 0.0)
        Q = num / np.sum(num)
        Q = np.maximum(Q, 1e-15)
        Q = Q / np.sum(Q)

        PQ_diff = (P - Q) * num
        grad = 4.0 * np.sum(PQ_diff[:, :, None] * diff, axis=1)
        Y -= lr * grad

    diff = Y[:, None, :] - Y[None, :, :]
    dist2 = np.sum(diff ** 2, axis=-1)
    num = 1.0 / (1.0 + dist2)
    np.fill_diagonal(num, 0.0)
    Q = num / np.sum(num)
    Q = np.maximum(Q, 1e-15)
    Q = Q / np.sum(Q)
    kl = float(np.sum(P * np.log(P / Q)))
    return Y, kl

    Case Study 1: Single-Cell RNA Sequencing & Trajectory Bifurcations

    • Context: An oncology laboratory analyzed \(12,000\) single-cell transcriptomes across \(18,500\) gene expression markers during hematopoiesis (stem cell differentiation into lymphoid vs. myeloid lineages).
    • The Linear PCA Failure: Because differentiation kinetics follow continuous curved branching manifolds, linear PCA projected the entire population into an undifferentiated spherical mass. The first two principal axes accounted for only \(11.4\%\) of variance, obscuring developmental lineages.
    • The Non-Linear Solution: Deploying UMAP with n_neighbors = 30 and min_dist = 0.2 preserved local geodesic trajectories. The resulting 2D embedding revealed a continuous Y-shaped bifurcating manifold with hematopoietic stem cells at the base transitioning smoothly into distinct lymphoid and myeloid lineages. Downstream RNA velocity vectors aligned seamlessly along the UMAP manifold paths [McInnes2018].

    Case Study 2: Hyperspectral Satellite Remote Sensing & Band Pruning

    • Context: An environmental monitoring constellation captures \(128\) contiguous radiometric channels from visible to shortwave infrared (\(400\text{ nm}\) to \(2500\text{ nm}\)).
    • The Problem: Downstream crop disease classification suffered from the curse of dimensionality (\(d = 128\)) and severe overfitting due to collinearity between adjacent spectral bands.
    • Spectral PCA Decomposition: SVD decomposition of the sample correlation matrix revealed that the first \(4\) principal components accounted for \(98.4\%\) of total spectral variance (\(\text{EVR}_4 = 0.984\)). The remaining \(124\) components had eigenvalues \(\lambda_j < 0.15\), representing sensor thermal noise and atmospheric scattering.
    • Outcome: Compressing the input pipeline to the top \(4\) principal components slashed downstream model inference latency by \(18\times\) while reducing out-of-domain classification error from \(16.2\%\) to \(4.1\%\) [Jolliffe2016].

    Case Study 3: Transaction Laundering Ring Detection via TDA Persistent Homology

    • Context: A global payments processor investigated synthetic identity theft where illicit funds were routed through layered shell accounts before returning to originators.
    • The Trap: Both PCA biplots and t-SNE embeddings failed: PCA collapsed the network into high-volume merchants, and t-SNE broke the network into disconnected clusters because local neighbor density was uniform across the graph.
    • Persistent Homology Application: By computing the Vietoris-Rips filtration over graph geodesic distances, the security team extracted Betti numbers. A persistent 1-dimensional topological cycle (\(\beta_1 = 1\)) emerged with lifespan \(\ell = d_{\text{death}} - b_{\text{birth}} = 3.82\), persisting across filtration scales. This persistent topological hole uncovered a 9-node circular money-laundering funnel that had evaded standard anomaly detection rules [Carlsson2009].

    ❓ Interactive Self-Assessment Micro-Checks

    Micro-Check 1: The Crowding Problem & Student-\(t\) Distribution

    Why does t-SNE employ a heavy-tailed Student-\(t\) distribution (Cauchy kernel) in the 2D target embedding space rather than a Gaussian distribution?

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Answer: In high-dimensional space \(\mathbb{R}^d\), the volume of a sphere of radius \(r\) scales as \(r^d\), whereas in 2D it scales only as \(r^2\). Moderate pairwise distances in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) require vastly more space than is available in 2D.

    If a Gaussian distribution \(\exp(-\|\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j\|^2)\) were used in 2D, the rapid exponential tail decay would force moderately distant points to experience crushing repulsive forces, collapsing disparate clusters onto the center of the plot (the crowding problem).

    The Student-\(t\) distribution with 1 degree of freedom has an inverse-square power-law tail \((1 + \|\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j\|^2)^{-1}\). This heavy tail allows points separated by moderate distances in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) to be placed much further apart in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) without incurring extreme probability mismatch penalties [VanDerMaaten2008].

    Micro-Check 2: Kaiser-Guttman Stopping Rule on Standardized Data

    When performing PCA on a standardized correlation matrix \(\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\), why does the Kaiser-Guttman rule set the component retention threshold to \(\lambda > 1.0\)?

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Answer: On standardized data, each feature is normalized to unit variance (\(s_j^2 = 1.0\)). The total variance of the dataset is equal to the trace of the correlation matrix: $\(\text{Tr}(\mathbf{R}) = \sum_{j=1}^d r_{jj} = d\)$ By the spectral decomposition, the sum of all eigenvalues equals the trace: $\(\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j = d \implies \bar{\lambda} = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j = 1.0\)$ Therefore, an eigenvalue \(\lambda_j < 1.0\) accounts for less variance than an individual raw feature. Retaining such components adds noise without providing compression benefits [Jolliffe2016].

    Micro-Check 3: Misinterpreting Inter-Cluster Distances in t-SNE vs. UMAP

    A junior analyst asserts: "In our t-SNE plot, Cluster A is 4 cm away from Cluster B, and Cluster C is 12 cm away, so Cluster C is three times more biochemically distant." Why is this assertion statistically invalid, and how does UMAP differ?

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Answer: In t-SNE, the target probability is localized: once points belong to distinct clusters, high-dimensional affinities \(p_{ij} \approx 0\). Because the attractive gradient is weighted by \(p_{ij}\), there is virtually zero attractive force between separated clusters. Their relative spatial positioning in the 2D plot is determined solely by the random initialization and uniform repulsion, making inter-cluster distance meaningless [Wattenberg2016].

    In contrast, UMAP's fuzzy cross-entropy objective contains an explicit global repulsion term \((1 - \mu_{ij}) \log(1 - \nu_{ij})^{-1}\) that acts continuously across all non-neighboring pairs. As a result, UMAP preserves global manifold topology and relative cluster distances substantially better than t-SNE [McInnes2018].

    Micro-Check 4: Persistent Homology & Betti Numbers in EDA

    How does a persistence diagram distinguish a genuine data cycle (e.g., a periodic cell cycle) from random sampling noise?

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Answer: In Persistent Homology, the filtration sweeps the proximity parameter \(\epsilon\) from \(0\) to \(\infty\). Each 1-dimensional cycle has a birth scale \(b_k\) and a death scale \(d_k\), plotted as a point \((b_k, d_k)\) in a persistence diagram.

    • Points situated close to the diagonal line \(d = b\) have a short lifespan \(\ell_k = d_k - b_k \approx 0\). These represent geometric sampling noise (temporary holes formed by sparse point spacing that are quickly filled by adjacent simplices).
    • Points situated far above the diagonal line have a long lifespan \(\ell_k \gg 0\). These persist across multiple geometric scales, proving the existence of a true topological hole or cyclical attractor in the data manifold [Carlsson2009].
