    🎯 Conceptual Overview & The Tri-Partite Mitigation Taxonomy

    Mitigating algorithmic bias requires intervention across distinct operational boundaries of the machine learning system lifecycle. When raw datasets inherit historical, sampling, or proxy discrimination, passive empirical risk minimization inevitably reproduces and reinforces that disparity.

    Engineering interventions are categorized into three fundamental stages (Kamiran & Calders, 2012; Agarwal et al., 2018; Hardt et al., 2016):

    📐 Stage 1: Pre-Processing Bias Mitigation

    Pre-processing operates directly on the empirical dataset \(\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, a_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N\) prior to model selection or training. Because it does not modify the downstream learner, pre-processing is model-agnostic, allowing any existing supervised learning framework to train without architectural redesign.

    1. Kamiran-Calders Sample Re-weighting (2012)

    Let \(A \in \{0, 1\}\) denote sensitive attribute and \(Y \in \{0, 1\}\) ground-truth target label. The uncorrected dataset exhibits statistical dependence if \(\mathbb{P}(A=a, Y=y) \ne \mathbb{P}(A=a)\mathbb{P}(Y=y)\).

    Kamiran and Calders formulated importance weights \(W(a, y)\) assigned to each training instance:

    \[W(a, y) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(A = a) \cdot \mathbb{P}(Y = y)}{\mathbb{P}(A = a, Y = y)}\]

    In finite empirical samples of size \(N\), let \(N_a = |\{i : a_i = a\}|\), \(N_y = |\{i : y_i = y\}|\), and \(N_{a, y} = |\{i : a_i = a, y_i = y\}|\):

    \[\boxed{W(a, y) = \frac{\frac{N_a}{N} \cdot \frac{N_y}{N}}{\frac{N_{a, y}}{N}} = \frac{N_a \cdot N_y}{N \cdot N_{a, y}}}\]

    Mathematical Invariant Proof:

    Under weighted empirical sampling, the expectation of joint demographic occurrence satisfies exact independence:

    \[\mathbb{E}_W\left[\mathbb{I}(A = a \wedge Y = y)\right] = \sum_{i: a_i=a, y_i=y} \frac{W(a, y)}{N} = N_{a, y} \cdot \frac{1}{N} \cdot \left(\frac{N_a N_y}{N N_{a, y}}\right) = \frac{N_a}{N} \cdot \frac{N_y}{N} = \mathbb{P}(A = a) \cdot \mathbb{P}(Y = y)\]

    Effect on Disparate Impact: Reweighting scales the effective loss incurred by under-represented favorable instances (e.g., approved minority applicants \(A=0, Y=1\)), forcing the subsequent empirical risk minimizer to treat positive outcomes as statistically independent of group membership.

    2. Disparate Impact Remover: Quantile CDF Inversion Repair (Feldman et al., 2015)

    When feature distributions differ across sensitive groups, models learn indirect proxies. The Disparate Impact Remover transforms numerical feature values \(X\) such that the conditional cumulative distribution functions \(F_{X \mid A=0}\) and \(F_{X \mid A=1}\) align into a shared reference distribution \(F_{\text{target}}\) (Feldman et al., 2015).

    For each continuous feature \(X\) and sensitive group \(a\): 1. Compute the empirical CDF within group \(a\): \(u_i = F_{X \mid A=a}(x_i) = \frac{1}{N_a} \sum_{j: a_j=a} \mathbb{I}(X_j \le x_i)\). 2. Compute the shared target distribution (typically the pooled median quantile function \(F_{\text{target}}^{-1}(u)\)). 3. Apply the Repair Level Parameter \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\):

    \[\boxed{\tilde{x}_i = (1 - \lambda) \cdot x_i + \lambda \cdot F_{\text{target}}^{-1}\left(F_{X \mid A=a_i}(x_i)\right)}\]
    • At \(\lambda = 0\): The feature distribution is unchanged (raw accuracy preserved, maximum proxy bias).
    • At \(\lambda = 1\): The feature distribution across both groups is identical (\(F_{\tilde{X} \mid A=0} \equiv F_{\tilde{X} \mid A=1}\)), guaranteeing that no downstream classifier can reconstruct sensitive attribute \(A\) from \(\tilde{X}\) (complete proxy elimination).

    ⚡ Stage 2: In-Processing Bias Mitigation

    In-processing interventions enforce fairness constraints directly during gradient optimization, achieving superior accuracy-fairness Pareto bounds compared to data-only or post-hoc threshold manipulations.

    1. Adversarial Debiasing & Orthogonal Gradient Projection (Zhang et al., 2018)

    Adversarial debiasing formulates fair training as a two-player zero-sum game between: - Predictor Network \(f_\theta\): Learns latent representation \(Z = h_\theta(X)\) and prediction \(\hat{Y} = \sigma(W Z)\). - Adversary Network \(g_\phi\): Attempts to predict sensitive attribute \(A\) from the representation \(Z\).

    The minimax objective is:

    \[\min_{\theta} \max_{\phi} \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}(f_\theta(X), Y) - \alpha \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}(g_\phi(h_\theta(X)), A)\]

  Ortho --> Update["Update Latent Weights θ"]:::blue

    Derivation of the Orthogonal Gradient Projection:

    Simple gradient subtraction (\(\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} - \alpha \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}\)) causes destructive gradient interference and training divergence. To preserve task utility while neutralizing the adversary, Zhang et al. project the primary task gradient orthogonal to the adversary's gradient:

    \[\text{proj}_u(v) = \frac{\langle v, u \rangle}{\|u\|_2^2} u\]
    \[\boxed{\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} - \frac{\langle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} \rangle}{\|\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}\|_2^2} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} - \alpha \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}}\]

    Mathematical Invariant: The modified task update vector \(v_{\perp} = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} - \text{proj}_{\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}}(\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}})\) satisfies: $\(\langle v_{\perp}, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} \rangle = \left\langle \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} - \frac{\langle \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}, \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} \rangle}{\|\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}\|^2} \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}, \; \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} \right\rangle = \langle \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}, \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} \rangle - \langle \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}, \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} \rangle = 0\)$

    The predictor updates its parameters exclusively along manifolds that are orthogonal to the sensitive information leakage direction, preventing representation collapse while retaining task predictive power.

    2. Fair Reductions via Exponentiated Gradients (Agarwal et al., 2018)

    Agarwal et al. demonstrated that fair classification subject to arbitrary linear constraints (Demographic Parity, Equalized Odds, Predictive Equality) reduces to a sequence of cost-sensitive classification sub-problems.

    Let \(\mathcal{H}\) be an unconstrained hypothesis class and \(Q \in \Delta(\mathcal{H})\) a probability distribution over hypotheses. The constrained problem is:

    \[\min_{Q \in \Delta(\mathcal{H})} \mathcal{E}(Q) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \hat{\mathbf{M}} Q \le \vec{c}\]

    where \(\hat{\mathbf{M}}\) is a matrix whose rows encode fairness constraints (e.g., selection rate differences across groups).

    Using Lagrangian duality, this is solved as a two-player zero-sum game:

    \[\min_{Q \in \Delta(\mathcal{H})} \max_{\vec{\lambda} \ge 0} \mathcal{L}(Q, \vec{\lambda}) = \mathcal{E}(Q) + \vec{\lambda}^T \left(\hat{\mathbf{M}} Q - \vec{c}\right)\]

    The Two-Player Algorithm:

    1. Regulator (Dual Player): Updates Lagrange multipliers \(\vec{\lambda}_t \in \mathbb{R}^K\) using multiplicative Exponentiated Gradient descent: $\(\lambda_{t+1, k} = \lambda_{t, k} \cdot \exp\left(\eta \cdot \left[\hat{\mathbf{M}} h_t - \vec{c}\right]_k\right)\)$
    2. Learner (Primal Player): Trains an unconstrained cost-sensitive classifier \(h_{t+1}\) on re-weighted sample costs induced by the current dual vector \(\vec{\lambda}_{t+1}\).
    3. Convergence Guarantee: Within \(O\left(\frac{\ln K}{\epsilon^2}\right)\) iterations, the randomized ensemble \(Q^* = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T h_t\) achieves \(\epsilon\)-approximate Pareto optimality.

    🎯 Stage 3: Post-Processing Bias Mitigation

    Post-processing methods operate strictly on continuous model outputs \(S(x) \in [0, 1]\) without requiring access to model parameters or internal training loops. They are vital when dealing with black-box proprietary APIs or regulated frozen models.

    1. Group-Specific ROC Convex Hull Optimization (Hardt et al., 2016)

    To satisfy Equalized Odds (\(\text{TPR}_0 = \text{TPR}_1\) and \(\text{FPR}_0 = \text{FPR}_1\)), a single global threshold \(\tau\) is insufficient whenever groups have different ROC geometries.

    For each sensitive group \(a \in \{0, 1\}\), construct the convex hull of its empirical ROC curve: $\(\mathcal{C}_a = \text{ConvexHull}\left(\left\{(\text{FPR}_a(\tau), \text{TPR}_a(\tau)) : \tau \in [0, 1]\right\} \cup \{(0, 0), (1, 1)\}\right)\)$

    The optimal equalized operating point \((F^*, T^*)\) is obtained via linear programming:

    \[\max_{(F, T) \in \mathcal{C}_0 \cap \mathcal{C}_1} \mathbb{P}(Y = 1) \cdot T - \mathbb{P}(Y = 0) \cdot F\]

    Once \((F^*, T^*)\) is located, group-specific thresholds \(\tau_0^*\) and \(\tau_1^*\) (and optional randomization weights \(p_{\text{rand}, a}\)) are extracted to produce calibrated binary allocations.

    2. Reject Option Classification (Kamiran et al., 2012)

    Reject Option Classification (ROC) targets predictions falling in the decision boundary's critical uncertainty margin: $\(\text{Margin}_\theta = \{x : |S(x) - 0.50| \le \theta\}\)$

    Instances outside the band retain standard threshold decisions. Within the boundary uncertainty band: - Unprivileged group applicants (\(A = 0\)) near the boundary are granted favorable classification (\(\hat{Y} = 1\)). - Privileged group applicants (\(A = 1\)) near the boundary are assigned conservative classification (\(\hat{Y} = 0\)).

    The optimal boundary width \(\theta^*\) is selected by grid search to maximize accuracy subject to \(\text{DIR} \ge 0.80\).

    """Schema governing Post-Processing Dual-Threshold Equalized Odds Tuning."""
    tau_group_0: float = Field(..., ge=0.0, le=1.0)
    tau_group_1: float = Field(..., ge=0.0, le=1.0)
    group_0_tpr: float = Field(..., ge=0.0, le=1.0)
    group_1_tpr: float = Field(..., ge=0.0, le=1.0)
    tpr_gap: float = Field(..., ge=0.0)
    average_odds_difference: float = Field(..., ge=0.0)
    equal_opportunity_satisfied: bool

def compute_kamiran_calders_weights(A: np.ndarray, Y: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """
    Computes importance sample weights W(a, y) = [P(A=a) * P(Y=y)] / P(A=a, Y=y)
    """
    N = len(A)
    weights = np.zeros(N, dtype=np.float64)
    for a_val in [0, 1]:
        for y_val in [0, 1]:
            mask = (A == a_val) & (Y == y_val)
            n_ay = np.sum(mask)
            n_a = np.sum(A == a_val)
            n_y = np.sum(Y == y_val)
            if n_ay > 0:
                w = (n_a * n_y) / (N * n_ay)
                weights[mask] = w
    return weights


def feldman_quantile_repair(
    X: np.ndarray, A: np.ndarray, repair_level: float = 1.0
) -> np.ndarray:
    """
    Applies Feldman Disparate Impact Remover via quantile inversion:
    X_repaired = (1 - lambda) * X + lambda * F_target^-1(F_a(X))
    """
    X_repaired = X.copy()
    for a_val in [0, 1]:
        idx = np.where(A == a_val)[0]
        if len(idx) == 0:
            continue
        # Empirical quantile rank
        ranks = np.argsort(np.argsort(X[idx])) / (len(idx) - 1 if len(idx) > 1 else 1)
        # Median reference target distribution across pooled data
        pooled_quantiles = np.quantile(X, ranks)
        X_repaired[idx] = (1.0 - repair_level) * X[idx] + repair_level * pooled_quantiles
    return X_repaired


def project_adversarial_gradient(
    grad_pred: np.ndarray, grad_adv: np.ndarray, alpha: float = 1.0
) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, float]:
    """
    Projects task gradient orthogonal to adversary gradient:
    v_total = grad_pred - proj_adv(grad_pred) - alpha * grad_adv
    """
    adv_norm_sq = float(np.sum(grad_adv ** 2))
    if adv_norm_sq < 1e-12:
        return grad_pred, 0.0

    inner_prod = float(np.dot(grad_pred, grad_adv))
    proj = (inner_prod / adv_norm_sq) * grad_adv
    ortho_component = grad_pred - proj
    grad_total = ortho_component - alpha * grad_adv

    ortho_check = float(np.dot(ortho_component, grad_adv))
    return grad_total, ortho_check


def optimize_dual_thresholds(
    scores: np.ndarray, A: np.ndarray, Y: np.ndarray, search_steps: int = 40
) -> PostProcessingThresholdContract:
    """
    Grid searches group thresholds (tau_0, tau_1) to minimize Equal Opportunity TPR gap.
    """
    best_tau0 = 0.5
    best_tau1 = 0.5
    min_gap = 1.0
    best_tpr0 = 0.0
    best_tpr1 = 0.0
    best_aod = 1.0

    s0_pos = scores[(A == 0) & (Y == 1)]
    s1_pos = scores[(A == 1) & (Y == 1)]
    s0_neg = scores[(A == 0) & (Y == 0)]
    s1_neg = scores[(A == 1) & (Y == 0)]

    for t0 in np.linspace(0.2, 0.8, search_steps):
        tpr0 = float(np.mean(s0_pos >= t0)) if len(s0_pos) > 0 else 0.0
        fpr0 = float(np.mean(s0_neg >= t0)) if len(s0_neg) > 0 else 0.0
        for t1 in np.linspace(0.2, 0.8, search_steps):
            tpr1 = float(np.mean(s1_pos >= t1)) if len(s1_pos) > 0 else 0.0
            fpr1 = float(np.mean(s1_neg >= t1)) if len(s1_neg) > 0 else 0.0

            gap = abs(tpr0 - tpr1)
            aod = 0.5 * (gap + abs(fpr0 - fpr1))
            if gap < min_gap:
                min_gap = gap
                best_tau0 = float(t0)
                best_tau1 = float(t1)
                best_tpr0 = tpr0
                best_tpr1 = tpr1
                best_aod = aod

    return PostProcessingThresholdContract(
        tau_group_0=round(best_tau0, 4),
        tau_group_1=round(best_tau1, 4),
        group_0_tpr=round(best_tpr0, 4),
        group_1_tpr=round(best_tpr1, 4),
        tpr_gap=round(min_gap, 4),
        average_odds_difference=round(best_aod, 4),
        equal_opportunity_satisfied=bool(min_gap <= 0.05),
    )

    ❓ Interactive Self-Assessment Micro-Checks

    Check 1: Exact Numerical Kamiran-Calders Weight Calculation

    In a university admissions dataset of \(N = 5000\) applicants: - Rural applicants (\(A = 0\)): \(1000\) total, of which \(200\) are admitted (\(Y = 1\)). - Urban applicants (\(A = 1\)): \(4000\) total, of which \(1800\) are admitted (\(Y = 1\)). Calculate the exact Kamiran-Calders sample weight \(W(A=0, Y=1)\) for an admitted rural applicant.

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Step 1: Calculate the marginal counts: - Total sample size: \(N = 5000\). - Group count for rural: \(N_a = 1000\). - Total admitted across all groups: \(N_y = 200 + 1800 = 2000\). - Observed joint count: \(N_{a, y} = 200\).

    Step 2: Apply the Kamiran-Calders formula: $\(W(A=0, Y=1) = \frac{N_a \cdot N_y}{N \cdot N_{a, y}} = \frac{1000 \cdot 2000}{5000 \cdot 200} = \frac{2,000,000}{1,000,000} = 2.00\)$

    Conclusion: Admitted rural applicants receive a sample importance weight of \(2.00\) (doubling their loss influence) to balance their empirical representation under independence.

    Check 2: Orthogonal Gradient Projection Geometry

    In adversarial debiasing, suppose the primary task gradient is \(\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} = [3.0, 4.0]^T\) and the adversary's gradient is \(\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} = [1.0, 0.0]^T\). Calculate the projected task gradient component \(v_{\perp}\) orthogonal to \(\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}\), and verify that its dot product with \(\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}\) is zero.

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Step 1: Compute the projection of \(\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}\) onto \(\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}\): $\(\text{proj}_u(v) = \frac{\langle v, u \rangle}{\|u\|^2} u\)$ Here \(u = [1.0, 0.0]^T \implies \|u\|^2 = 1.0^2 + 0.0^2 = 1.0\). The dot product \(\langle v, u \rangle = (3.0)(1.0) + (4.0)(0.0) = 3.0\). $\(\text{proj}_{\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}}}(\nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}) = \frac{3.0}{1.0} \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 \\ 0.0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.0 \\ 0.0 \end{bmatrix}\)$

    Step 2: Compute the orthogonal component \(v_{\perp}\): $\(v_{\perp} = \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} - \text{proj} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.0 \\ 4.0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 3.0 \\ 0.0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.0 \\ 4.0 \end{bmatrix}\)$

    Step 3: Verify orthogonality: $\(\langle v_{\perp}, \nabla \mathcal{L}_{\text{adv}} \rangle = (0.0)(1.0) + (4.0)(0.0) = 0.0\)$ The gradient component along the adversary's detection axis is completely eliminated, allowing optimization strictly in the adversary-invariant subspace.

    Check 3: Reject Option Classification Trade-Offs

    Under Reject Option Classification (ROC), what failure mode occurs if the uncertainty margin \(\theta\) is set too wide (e.g., \(\theta = 0.40\))?

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    If the margin \(\theta\) is set excessively wide: 1. Excessive Policy Distortion: A massive proportion of predictions (\(|S(x) - 0.50| \le 0.40 \implies S(x) \in [0.10, 0.90]\)) fall into the override zone. 2. False Favorable Inversion: Low-scoring candidates (\(S(x) \approx 0.15\)) from the unprivileged group are flipped to positive outcomes, while highly qualified candidates (\(S(x) \approx 0.85\)) from the privileged group are denied. 3. Catastrophic Accuracy Collapse: The model ceases to function as a predictive tool, generating extreme false positive rates for group 0 and extreme false negative rates for group 1. 4. Production Rule: The threshold margin \(\theta\) must be restricted to the boundary neighborhood (\(\theta \le 0.10\)) where score confidence is genuinely ambiguous.

    Check 4: Quantile Inversion Distribution Repair vs. Disparate Impact Restoration

    In Feldman et al.'s quantile distribution repair algorithm, suppose a numerical credit feature \(X\) has group-conditional cumulative distributions \(F_0(x)\) for minority group \(A=0\) and \(F_1(x)\) for majority group \(A=1\).

    1. Define the repair transformation \(\tilde{X}\) with partial repair parameter \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\).
    2. Explain why setting \(\lambda = 1.0\) completely eliminates the ability of any downstream classifier to predict sensitive attribute \(A\) from \(\tilde{X}\).
    Reveal Detailed Solution & Proof

    1. Partial Quantile Repair Formula: Let \(F_{\text{target}}^{-1}(q)\) be the median quantile function across groups: \(F_{\text{target}}^{-1}(q) = \text{median}_{a \in \{0, 1\}} F_a^{-1}(q)\). The partially repaired feature \(\tilde{X}\) for an individual with attribute \(A=a\) and feature value \(X=x\) is: $\(\tilde{X} = (1 - \lambda) x + \lambda F_{\text{target}}^{-1}(F_a(x))\)$ where \(F_a(x)\) computes the individual's percentile within their own demographic group.

    2. Full Repair (\(\lambda = 1.0\)) Information Erasure Proof: When \(\lambda = 1.0\): $\(\tilde{X} = F_{\text{target}}^{-1}(F_a(X))\)$ By the probability integral transform, for any continuous random variable \(X \mid A=a\), the percentile \(Q = F_a(X)\) is uniformly distributed on \([0, 1]\), completely independent of \(A\): $\(\mathbb{P}(Q \le q \mid A = a) = q, \quad \forall a \in \{0, 1\}\)$ Since \(F_{\text{target}}^{-1}\) is a deterministic monotonic mapping applied identically across all groups, the distribution of \(\tilde{X} \mid A=0\) and \(\tilde{X} \mid A=1\) are identical: $\(\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X} \le t \mid A = 0) = \mathbb{P}(\tilde{X} \le t \mid A = 1) = F_{\text{target}}(t)\)$ Therefore, \(\tilde{X} \perp A\). By the Data Processing Inequality, no downstream model (linear or non-linear) can extract mutual information about \(A\) from \(\tilde{X}\), guaranteeing that mutual information \(I(\tilde{X}; A) = 0\).
