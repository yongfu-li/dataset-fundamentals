    🎯 Conceptual Overview & Socio-Technical Feedback Loops

    Data is not an objective mirror of physical reality; it is an artifact produced by institutional measurement processes, sampling methodologies, historical resource allocations, and socio-economic inequities [Barocas2016]. When statistical estimators and machine learning models train on uncorrected observational datasets, they do not merely reflect existing social disparities—they systematically codify, accelerate, and institutionalize them through self-reinforcing feedback loops [Buolamwini2018, Hardt2016], necessitating auditing frameworks like AIF360 [Bellamy2019] and standardized provenance documentation through datasheets [Gebru2021].

    1. The Socio-Technical Feedback Dynamical System

    Let \(X_t\) denote the observed empirical distribution at discrete operational round \(t\). When an algorithmic system allocates enforcement, monitoring, auditing, or capital based on an estimator \(f_{\theta_t}\), the subsequent observation process at \(t + 1\) becomes conditionally dependent on the model's own past decisions:

    \[\Pr(X_{t+1} \mid X_t, f_{\theta_t}) \neq \Pr(X_{t+1} \mid X_t)\]

    Mathematical Formulation of Runaway Sampling Loops

    Consider two geographic districts \(A\) and \(B\) with identical true latent crime rates \(\lambda_A = \lambda_B = \lambda\). Suppose historical dispatch records contain an initial measurement or reporting over-count in District \(A\):

    \[\hat{\lambda}_A(0) = (1 + \delta) \cdot \lambda, \quad \hat{\lambda}_B(0) = \lambda \quad (\delta > 0)\]

    Let patrol resources be allocated dynamically between the districts. The proportion of patrols dispatched to District \(A\), denoted \(\pi_A(t) \in [0, 1]\), follows a power-law softmax response to observed crime density with feedback exponent \(\gamma > 0\):

    \[\pi_A(t) = \frac{\hat{\lambda}_A(t)^\gamma}{\hat{\lambda}_A(t)^\gamma + \hat{\lambda}_B(t)^\gamma}, \quad \pi_B(t) = 1 - \pi_A(t)\]

    The detected crime rate at operational round \(t\) scales directly with enforcement presence, where \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) represents baseline citizen reporting independent of police patrols:

    \[\hat{\lambda}_A(t) = \lambda \cdot \left[ \alpha + (1 - \alpha) \pi_A(t) \right], \quad \hat{\lambda}_B(t) = \lambda \cdot \left[ \alpha + (1 - \alpha) (1 - \pi_A(t)) \right]\]

    Analytical Derivation: Fixed-Point Stability and Pitchfork Bifurcation

    Substituting detected rates into the patrol allocation yields a 1D discrete dynamical map \(x_{t+1} = T(x_t)\), where \(x_t = \pi_A(t) \in [0, 1]\):

    \[T(x) = \frac{\left[ \alpha + (1 - \alpha) x \right]^\gamma}{\left[ \alpha + (1 - \alpha) x \right]^\gamma + \left[ \alpha + (1 - \alpha) (1 - x) \right]^\gamma}\]

    By symmetry, \(x^* = \frac{1}{2}\) is always a stationary fixed point (\(T(1/2) = 1/2\)). To evaluate its asymptotic stability, we differentiate \(T(x)\) at \(x^* = \frac{1}{2}\). Let \(u(x) = \alpha + (1 - \alpha)x\), so \(u(1/2) = \frac{1 + \alpha}{2}\) and \(u'(x) = 1 - \alpha\). Let \(f(x) = u(x)^\gamma\) and \(g(x) = u(1-x)^\gamma\).

    Using the quotient rule:

    \[T'(x) = \frac{f'(x) g(x) - f(x) g'(x)}{[f(x) + g(x)]^2}\]

    At \(x = 1/2\), \(f(1/2) = g(1/2) = u(1/2)^\gamma\), and \(g'(1/2) = -f'(1/2)\):

    \[T'\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{f' f - f(-f')}{(2f)^2} = \frac{2 f f'}{4 f^2} = \frac{f'\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2 f\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)}\]

    Substituting \(f'\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \gamma u(1/2)^{\gamma - 1} (1 - \alpha)\):

    \[T'\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{\gamma u(1/2)^{\gamma - 1} (1 - \alpha)}{2 u(1/2)^\gamma} = \frac{\gamma (1 - \alpha)}{2 u(1/2)} = \frac{\gamma (1 - \alpha)}{2 \left( \frac{1 + \alpha}{2} \right)} = \gamma \frac{1 - \alpha}{1 + \alpha}\]

    [!IMPORTANT] The Supercritical Pitchfork Bifurcation Theorem: - If \(\gamma < \frac{1 + \alpha}{1 - \alpha}\), then \(|T'(1/2)| < 1\). The parity fixed point \(x^* = 1/2\) is locally asymptotically stable. Any initial reporting distortion \(\delta > 0\) decays exponentially: \(\lim_{t \to \infty} \pi_A(t) = 0.5\). - If \(\gamma > \frac{1 + \alpha}{1 - \alpha}\), then \(T'(1/2) > 1\). The parity fixed point undergoes a supercritical pitchfork bifurcation and becomes unstable. Any arbitrarily small perturbation \(\delta > 0\) triggers exponential divergence towards an absorbing boundary: $\(\lim_{t \to \infty} \pi_A(t) = 1.0, \quad \lim_{t \to \infty} \pi_B(t) = 0.0\)$ The algorithm allocates \(100\%\) of patrol units to District \(A\), manufacturing a synthetic crime wave from pure historical reporting bias [Ensign2018].

    2. Legal Protections: Disparate Treatment vs. Disparate Impact

    Under modern legal jurisprudence (US Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA), and EU AI Act Article 10), discrimination is classified into two distinct legal doctrines [Barocas2016]:

    Legal Standard Legal Definition Formal Mathematical Criteria Engineering Manifestation Remediation Strategy
    Disparate Treatment Intentional differential treatment explicitly conditioned on protected demographic attribute \(A\). \(f(\mathbf{x}, A = 0) \neq f(\mathbf{x}, A = 1)\) Including race, gender, or religion directly as model features. Blinding (dropping protected attribute columns from input matrix).
    Disparate Impact Facially neutral policies that produce disproportionately adverse outcomes for a protected class. \(\frac{\Pr(f(\mathbf{X}) = 1 \mid A = 0)}{\Pr(f(\mathbf{X}) = 1 \mid A = 1)} < 0.80\) (The 80% Rule) Excluding protected attribute \(A\), but retaining high-capacity proxy features (ZIP code, browsing metadata). Disparate impact mitigation: pre-processing reweighing, in-processing adversarial debiasing, or post-processing threshold adjustment.

    Formal Fairness Metric Definitions

    Let \(A \in \{0, 1\}\) denote the protected demographic attribute (where \(A=0\) is unprivileged and \(A=1\) is privileged), \(Y \in \{0, 1\}\) denote the true binary outcome, and \(\hat{Y} = f(\mathbf{X}) \in \{0, 1\}\) denote the model decision.

    1. Disparate Impact Ratio (DIR / Four-Fifths Rule): $\(\text{DIR} = \frac{\Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0)}{\Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1)}\)$ Under EEOC guidelines, a selection process exhibits actionable adverse impact if \(\text{DIR} < 0.80\) (\(80\%\)).

    2. Statistical Parity Difference (Demographic Parity): $\(\Delta_{\text{DP}} = \Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0) - \Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1)\)$ Measures absolute acceptance gap. Perfect demographic parity requires \(\Delta_{\text{DP}} = 0\), guaranteeing independence: \(\hat{Y} \perp A\).

    3. Equal Opportunity Difference: $\(\Delta_{\text{Eopp}} = \Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0, Y = 1) - \Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1, Y = 1) = \text{TPR}_0 - \text{TPR}_1\)$ Requires equal True Positive Rates among qualified individuals across groups [Hardt2016].

    4. Equalized Odds (Average Absolute Odds Difference): $\(\Delta_{\text{AOD}} = \frac{1}{2} \left( |\text{FPR}_0 - \text{FPR}_1| + |\text{TPR}_0 - \text{TPR}_1| \right)\)$ Demands joint conditional independence: \(\hat{Y} \perp A \mid Y\).

    5. Group Calibration (Predictive Parity): $\(\Pr(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = 0) = \Pr(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = 1) \iff \text{PPV}_0 = \text{PPV}_1\)$ Ensures that an assigned risk score or positive prediction reflects the same empirical likelihood of success regardless of group membership [Chouldechova2017].

    3. The Impossibility Theorem of Algorithmic Fairness

    A central theoretical breakthrough in algorithmic fairness is the mathematical impossibility of simultaneously satisfying Calibration within groups and Equalized Odds (or Equal False Positive Rates) when base rates differ across demographic groups [Kleinberg2016, Chouldechova2017].

    Theorem Formulation & Algebraic Proof

    Let \(p_g = \Pr(Y = 1 \mid A = g)\) denote the base prevalence rate of group \(g \in \{0, 1\}\). Let \(\text{PPV}_g = \Pr(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = g)\), \(\text{TPR}_g = \Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = g)\), and \(\text{FPR}_g = \Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 0, A = g)\).

    By Bayes' theorem, the joint probability of true positive prediction is:

    \[\Pr(\hat{Y} = 1, Y = 1 \mid A = g) = \text{PPV}_g \cdot \Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = g) = \text{TPR}_g \cdot p_g\]

    Hence:

    \[\Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = g) = \frac{\text{TPR}_g \cdot p_g}{\text{PPV}_g}\]

    Similarly, the joint probability of false positive prediction is:

    \[\Pr(\hat{Y} = 1, Y = 0 \mid A = g) = (1 - \text{PPV}_g) \cdot \Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = g) = \text{FPR}_g \cdot (1 - p_g)\]

    Substituting \(\Pr(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = g)\) from the true positive equation:

    \[(1 - \text{PPV}_g) \left( \frac{\text{TPR}_g \cdot p_g}{\text{PPV}_g} \right) = \text{FPR}_g \cdot (1 - p_g)\]

    Solving directly for False Positive Rate \(\text{FPR}_g\):

    \[\text{FPR}_g = \left( \frac{p_g}{1 - p_g} \right) \left( \frac{1 - \text{PPV}_g}{\text{PPV}_g} \right) \cdot \text{TPR}_g\]

    Now, suppose an algorithm enforces equal Group Calibration (\(\text{PPV}_0 = \text{PPV}_1 = v\)) and Equal Opportunity (equal true positive rates \(\text{TPR}_0 = \text{TPR}_1 = \tau > 0\)). Taking the ratio of False Positive Rates across groups:

    \[\frac{\text{FPR}_0}{\text{FPR}_1} = \frac{\left( \frac{p_0}{1 - p_0} \right) \left( \frac{1 - v}{v} \right) \tau}{\left( \frac{p_1}{1 - p_1} \right) \left( \frac{1 - v}{v} \right) \tau} = \frac{\frac{p_0}{1 - p_0}}{\frac{p_1}{1 - p_1}} = \frac{\text{Odds}(p_0)}{\text{Odds}(p_1)}\]

    [!WARNING] The Algorithmic Trilemma: If the underlying demographic groups exhibit unequal base prevalence rates (\(p_0 \neq p_1\)), then: $\(\frac{\text{FPR}_0}{\text{FPR}_1} = \frac{\text{Odds}(p_0)}{\text{Odds}(p_1)} \neq 1\)$ It is mathematically impossible to achieve both Equal Calibration (Equal PPV) and Equal False Positive Rates (FPR parity). Any classifier that achieves equal predictive value across groups with unequal base rates will inevitably produce higher false positive rates in the lower base-rate group, or higher false negative rates in the higher base-rate group [Kleinberg2016].

    Deconstructing the COMPAS Recidivism Controversy

    In 2016, ProPublica audited Northpointe's COMPAS risk assessment tool used in bail and sentencing hearings across the United States [Angwin2016]: - ProPublica's Finding: Black defendants who never recidivated were falsely classified as high-risk at almost double the rate of White defendants (\(\text{FPR}_{\text{Black}} = 44.9\%\) vs. \(\text{FPR}_{\text{White}} = 23.5\%\)). - Northpointe's Defense: The algorithm is fair because risk scores are well-calibrated; a risk score of 7 reflects the same \(\approx 60\%\) probability of recidivism regardless of race (\(\text{PPV}_{\text{Black}} \approx \text{PPV}_{\text{White}}\)).

    Both parties were mathematically correct. Because historical arrest base rates differed between cohorts in Broward County (\(p_{\text{Black}} \approx 51.5\%\) vs. \(p_{\text{White}} \approx 39.0\%\)), Kleinberg's theorem proves that Northpointe's calibration mathematically mandated the false positive disparity that ProPublica uncovered.

    4. Allocative Harms vs. Representational Harms

    Fairness harms in modern machine learning systems fall into two fundamental epistemological classes:

    HarmTaxonomy --> Representational["Representational Harms<br/>(Dignity, Identity & Perceptual Status)"]

    1. Allocative Harms: Occur when an algorithm withholds or differentially distributes immediate, measurable economic opportunities, goods, or liberties. They are quantifiable at decision time via confusion matrix disparities and disparate impact ratios.
    2. Representational Harms: Occur when an algorithmic system degrades the social standing, cultural dignity, or visibility of a demographic group. These harms accumulate diffusely over time, entrenching stereotypes without an explicit single-instance economic transaction [Barocas2016, Buolamwini2018].

# Production Implementation & Pydantic V2 Verification Suite for Lesson 7.1

from typing import Dict, List, Any
import numpy as np
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field, field_validator

    is_supercritical: bool = Field(..., description="True if T'(1/2) > 1 indicating unstable divergence")

    divergence_confirmed: bool = Field(..., description="Validates whether empirical simulation matches dynamical theory")

    base_rate_0: float = Field(..., gt=0.0, lt=1.0, description="Base prevalence rate of Group 0")
    base_rate_1: float = Field(..., gt=0.0, lt=1.0, description="Base prevalence rate of Group 1")
    calibrated_ppv: float = Field(..., gt=0.0, lt=1.0, description="Equal PPV enforced across both groups")
    equal_tpr: float = Field(..., gt=0.0, lt=1.0, description="Equal TPR enforced across both groups")
    fpr_0: float = Field(..., ge=0.0, le=1.0, description="Resulting False Positive Rate for Group 0")
    fpr_1: float = Field(..., ge=0.0, le=1.0, description="Resulting False Positive Rate for Group 1")
    empirical_fpr_ratio: float = Field(..., description="Empirical ratio of FPR_0 / FPR_1")
    theoretical_odds_ratio: float = Field(..., description="Theoretical Odds(p0) / Odds(p1) ratio")
    trilemma_proven: bool = Field(..., description="Validates that FPR ratio matches theoretical odds ratio")

class FairnessComplianceAudit(BaseModel):
    selection_rate_unprivileged: float = Field(..., ge=0.0, le=1.0)
    selection_rate_privileged: float = Field(..., ge=0.0, le=1.0)
    disparate_impact_ratio: float = Field(..., ge=0.0)
    demographic_parity_gap: float = Field(...)
    violates_four_fifths_rule: bool

def audit_feedback_dynamics(n_rounds: int = 50, initial_bias: float = 0.05, 
                           gamma: float = 1.80, alpha: float = 0.20) -> FeedbackLoopAudit:
    # 1. Closed-form derivative at x* = 1/2
    t_prime = gamma * (1.0 - alpha) / (1.0 + alpha)
    is_super = t_prime > 1.0

    # 2. Iterate discrete dynamical map
    obs_a = 10.0 * (1.0 + initial_bias)
    obs_b = 10.0
    for _ in range(n_rounds):
        w_a = obs_a ** gamma
        w_b = obs_b ** gamma
        pi_a = w_a / (w_a + w_b)
        obs_a = 10.0 * (alpha + (1.0 - alpha) * pi_a)
        obs_b = 10.0 * (alpha + (1.0 - alpha) * (1.0 - pi_a))

    div_confirmed = (pi_a > 0.85) if is_super else (abs(pi_a - 0.5) < 0.05)
    return FeedbackLoopAudit(
        initial_bias=initial_bias,
        gamma=gamma,
        alpha=alpha,
        theoretical_t_prime=round(t_prime, 4),
        is_supercritical=is_super,
        final_patrol_share_a=round(float(pi_a), 4),
        divergence_confirmed=div_confirmed
    )

def audit_impossibility_theorem(p0: float = 0.32, p1: float = 0.55, 
                               ppv: float = 0.68, tpr: float = 0.72) -> ImpossibilityTheoremAudit:
    # Closed-form FPR equation: FPR = (p / (1 - p)) * ((1 - PPV) / PPV) * TPR
    fpr0 = (p0 / (1.0 - p0)) * ((1.0 - ppv) / ppv) * tpr
    fpr1 = (p1 / (1.0 - p1)) * ((1.0 - ppv) / ppv) * tpr

    odds0 = p0 / (1.0 - p0)
    odds1 = p1 / (1.0 - p1)
    theo_odds_ratio = odds0 / odds1
    emp_fpr_ratio = fpr0 / fpr1

    trilemma_match = abs(emp_fpr_ratio - theo_odds_ratio) < 1e-12
    return ImpossibilityTheoremAudit(
        base_rate_0=p0,
        base_rate_1=p1,
        calibrated_ppv=ppv,
        equal_tpr=tpr,
        fpr_0=round(float(fpr0), 4),
        fpr_1=round(float(fpr1), 4),
        empirical_fpr_ratio=round(float(emp_fpr_ratio), 4),
        theoretical_odds_ratio=round(float(theo_odds_ratio), 4),
        trilemma_proven=trilemma_match
    )

def audit_fairness_compliance(hired_0: int, total_0: int, 
                              hired_1: int, total_1: int) -> FairnessComplianceAudit:
    sr0 = hired_0 / total_0
    sr1 = hired_1 / total_1
    dir_val = sr0 / sr1
    dp_gap = sr0 - sr1
    violates = dir_val < 0.80

    return FairnessComplianceAudit(
        selection_rate_unprivileged=round(sr0, 4),
        selection_rate_privileged=round(sr1, 4),
        disparate_impact_ratio=round(dir_val, 4),
        demographic_parity_gap=round(dp_gap, 4),
        violates_four_fifths_rule=violates
    )

    🔬 Empirical Case Study: COMPAS Bail Scoring & Amazon Resume Screening

    1. COMPAS Recidivism Risk Scoring (Angwin et al. / ProPublica, 2016):
    2. Context: The Northpointe COMPAS algorithm scored criminal defendants' likelihood of re-arrest on a 1-to-10 scale.
    3. Audit Findings: ProPublica's statistical audit revealed that while overall accuracy and calibration were comparable across racial groups, false positive rates were radically disparate: Black defendants who never re-offended were falsely predicted to recidivate at nearly twice the rate of White non-reoffenders (\(44.9\%\) vs. \(23.5\%\)). Conversely, White defendants who did re-offend were flagged as low-risk at almost double the rate of Black re-offenders (\(47.7\%\) vs. \(28.0\%\)).
    4. Structural Root: As proved algebraically above, because historical arrest rates differed between racial groups in the county, maintaining equal calibration across groups mathematically forced false positive disparities [Chouldechova2017].
    5. Amazon's Automated Resume Screener (2018):
    6. Context: Amazon engineers trained deep learning models on ten years of engineering resumes submitted to the company to automate applicant triage.
    7. The Failure Mode: Because the tech industry had been historically male-dominated, the training dataset contained a heavy skew toward male resumes. The model internalized this historical correlation, penalizing resumes containing the word "women's" (e.g., "women's rugby team captain") and downgrading graduates of all-women colleges.
    8. Outcome: Dropping explicitly gendered terms failed because high-dimensional latent vectors reconstructed the proxy. Amazon formally dismantled the tool in 2018.

    ❓ Interactive Self-Assessment Micro-Checks

    Self-Assessment 1: Why Does 'Fairness Through Unawareness' Fail Mathematically?

    Explain why simply removing the protected attribute \(A\) (e.g., race or gender) from the feature matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) fails to prevent disparate impact in downstream machine learning predictions.

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Answer: In modern high-dimensional datasets, non-protected features \(\mathbf{X}_{\setminus A}\) share extensive mutual information with \(A\).

    Even when \(A\) is omitted, an unconstrained empirical risk minimizer reconstructs an optimal Bayes estimate \(\hat{A} = g(\mathbf{X}_{\setminus A})\) via redundant proxy features: - Geographic identifiers (ZIP codes, census tracts) correlate strongly with race due to historical residential segregation. - Educational background, organizational memberships, browsing telemetry, and vocabulary act as high-capacity latent proxies.

    If the target label \(Y\) is correlated with \(A\) in historical data, the model will exploit \(\hat{A}\) through these proxies to optimize predictive loss, generating identical disparate impact [Dwork2012, Barocas2016].

    Self-Assessment 2: Calculating the EEOC Four-Fifths Rule

    In a corporate hiring pipeline, \(100\) men apply and \(40\) are hired (selection rate \(40\%\)). \(50\) women apply and \(15\) are hired (selection rate \(30\%\)). Calculate the Disparate Impact Ratio and determine if adverse impact exists under the EEOC 80% Rule.

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Answer: - Male Selection Rate (Privileged Group \(A=1\)): \(\Pr(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1) = 40 / 100 = 0.40\) - Female Selection Rate (Unprivileged Group \(A=0\)): \(\Pr(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0) = 15 / 50 = 0.30\) - Disparate Impact Ratio: $\(\text{DIR} = \frac{0.30}{0.40} = 0.75 \quad (75\%)\)$

    Because \(\text{DIR} = 75\% < 80\%\), the hiring procedure violates the EEOC Four-Fifths Rule, creating a prima facie evidentiary presumption of adverse disparate impact under Title VII.

    Self-Assessment 3: The Supercritical Pitchfork Bifurcation in Predictive Policing

    Suppose a predictive policing allocation algorithm operates with citizen baseline reporting \(\alpha = 0.333\) (so \(\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha} = 0.50\)). If the software vendor sets the feedback allocation exponent to \(\gamma = 2.4\), calculate \(T'(1/2)\) and predict the long-term operational outcome if District A begins with a \(2\%\) initial measurement bias.

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Answer: - The derivative at parity is: $\(T'\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \gamma \frac{1 - \alpha}{1 + \alpha} = 2.4 \times \frac{1 - 0.333}{1 + 0.333} = 2.4 \times \frac{0.667}{1.333} = 2.4 \times 0.50 = 1.20\)$ - Because \(T'(1/2) = 1.20 > 1.0\), the parity equilibrium \(x^* = 1/2\) is unstable. - The initial \(2\%\) distortion will exponentially amplify across rounds, leading to runaway divergence: \(\lim_{t \to \infty} \pi_A(t) = 1.0\). District A will receive \(100\%\) of patrol dispatches, creating a self-fulfilling institutional perception of a localized crime wave.

    Self-Assessment 4: The Kleinberg Impossibility Formula

    Suppose Group 0 has base rate \(p_0 = 0.20\) and Group 1 has base rate \(p_1 = 0.50\). An algorithm achieves equal positive predictive value \(\text{PPV}_0 = \text{PPV}_1\) and equal true positive rates \(\text{TPR}_0 = \text{TPR}_1\). Calculate the theoretical ratio of False Positive Rates \(\frac{\text{FPR}_0}{\text{FPR}_1}\).

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    Answer: - By Kleinberg's Theorem, the ratio of False Positive Rates under equal calibration and equal opportunity equals the base rate odds ratio: $\(\text{Odds}(p_0) = \frac{0.20}{1 - 0.20} = \frac{0.20}{0.80} = 0.25\)$ $\(\text{Odds}(p_1) = \frac{0.50}{1 - 0.50} = \frac{0.50}{0.50} = 1.00\)$ $\(\frac{\text{FPR}_0}{\text{FPR}_1} = \frac{\text{Odds}(p_0)}{\text{Odds}(p_1)} = \frac{0.25}{1.00} = 0.25\)$ - Group 0 will experience a False Positive Rate that is only \(25\%\) of Group 1's False Positive Rate (or conversely, Group 1's FPR is \(4\times\) higher). Parity of error rates is algebraically impossible.
