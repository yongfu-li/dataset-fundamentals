    🎯 Conceptual Overview & The Inherent Impossibility Theorem

    A pervasive misconception in applied machine learning assumes that given sufficiently large datasets, deep neural architectures, and optimal feature engineering, an algorithmic predictor can simultaneously satisfy all intuitive concepts of fairness. In 2016 and 2017, foundational mathematical theorems proved that the three primary families of group fairness—Independence (Demographic Parity), Separation (Equalized Odds), and Sufficiency (Predictive Calibration)—are mutually exclusive whenever demographic groups exhibit unequal ground-truth base rates (Kleinberg et al., 2017; Chouldechova, 2017).

    Fairness is not a single engineering optimization constraint to be solved in a loss function; it represents a set of contradictory normative ethical philosophies. An algorithm cannot simultaneously satisfy an individual's right to group-blind calibrated scoring and a group's right to equalized erroneous accusation rates. Engineering teams deploying high-stakes decision systems must navigate this trilemma deliberately, establishing explicit ethical and legal trade-offs.

    📐 The Three Pillars of Group Fairness

    Let \(X \in \mathbb{R}^d\) represent observed feature vectors, \(A \in \{0, 1\}\) denote a binary sensitive protected attribute (e.g., race, gender, disability status), \(Y \in \{0, 1\}\) the ground-truth outcome label, and \(\hat{Y} \in \{0, 1\}\) or continuous score \(R = f(X) \in [0, 1]\) the algorithmic prediction.

    The entire literature of statistical group fairness organizes around three conditional independence properties between the sensitive attribute \(A\), the true label \(Y\), and the score \(R\) (Barocas & Selbst, 2016):

    1. Pillar 1: Independence (Demographic Parity)

    A continuous score \(R\) or binary decision \(\hat{Y}\) satisfies Independence (or Demographic Parity) if the distribution of decisions is statistically independent of sensitive attribute \(A\):

    \[R \perp A \iff \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0) = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1)\]

    Regulatory & Mathematical Quantifications:

    1. Statistical Parity Difference (\(\Delta_{\text{DP}}\)): $\(\Delta_{\text{DP}} = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0) - \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1)\)$ Parity requires \(\Delta_{\text{DP}} = 0\).

    2. Disparate Impact Ratio (\(\text{DIR}\)): $\(\text{DIR} = \frac{\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0)}{\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1)}\)$ The United States Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) enforces the Four-Fifths (80%) Rule: any selection system with \(\text{DIR} < 0.80\) is prima facie unlawful disparate impact discrimination under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act.

    Philosophical Premise: "We are all equal." Any observed differences in historical outcomes \(Y\) reflect systemic societal oppression, historical discrimination, or biased labeling. An equitable system must distribute opportunities at equal rates across groups regardless of historical disparities.

    2. Pillar 2: Separation (Equalized Odds & Equality of Opportunity)

    A predictor satisfies Separation if the score \(R\) or decision \(\hat{Y}\) is conditionally independent of sensitive attribute \(A\) given the true outcome label \(Y\) (Hardt et al., 2016):

    \[R \perp A \mid Y \iff \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = y, A = 0) = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = y, A = 1), \quad \forall y \in \{0, 1\}\]

    Separation splits into two distinct error rate balance constraints:

    Condition A: True Positive Rate Parity (Equality of Opportunity)

    $\(\text{TPR}_0 = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = 0) = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = 1) = \text{TPR}_1\)$ Equivalently, False Negative Rates are equal: \(\text{FNR}_0 = \text{FNR}_1\).

    Condition B: False Positive Rate Parity (Predictive Equality)

    \[\text{FPR}_0 = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 0, A = 0) = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 0, A = 1) = \text{FPR}_1\]

    The composite Average Odds Difference (\(\Delta_{\text{AOD}}\)) measures total deviation from Equalized Odds: $\(\Delta_{\text{AOD}} = \frac{1}{2} \left( |\text{FPR}_0 - \text{FPR}_1| + |\text{TPR}_0 - \text{TPR}_1| \right)\)$

    Philosophical Premise: Individuals who are equally qualified (or equally innocent) should experience identical probabilities of success (or erroneous punishment), irrespective of demographic group membership.

    3. Pillar 3: Sufficiency (Predictive Parity & Calibration within Groups)

    A score \(R = f(X) \in [0, 1]\) satisfies Sufficiency if the ground-truth outcome \(Y\) is conditionally independent of sensitive attribute \(A\) given score \(R\) (Pleiss et al., 2017):

    \[Y \perp A \mid R \iff \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid R = r, A = 0) = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid R = r, A = 1) = r, \quad \forall r \in [0, 1]\]

    For binary decision rules \(\hat{Y} \in \{0, 1\}\), Sufficiency requires equal Positive Predictive Value (PPV) and equal Negative Predictive Value (NPV):

    \[\text{PPV}_0 = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = 0) = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = 1) = \text{PPV}_1$$ $$\text{NPV}_0 = \mathbb{P}(Y = 0 \mid \hat{Y} = 0, A = 0) = \mathbb{P}(Y = 0 \mid \hat{Y} = 0, A = 1) = \text{NPV}_1\]

    Philosophical Premise: "A score of 70% must mean 70% risk for everyone." If a credit scoring model assigns an applicant a 30% default probability, that applicant's expected repayment reliability should not depend on their race or gender. Group-specific calibration ensures decisions remain statistically trustworthy to decision-makers.

    🔬 Rigorous Derivation: The Kleinberg-Chouldechova Impossibility Theorem

    We now present the complete algebraic proof establishing that Separation and Sufficiency cannot simultaneously hold when demographic groups exhibit unequal ground-truth base rates, except under degenerate conditions (Chouldechova, 2017; Kleinberg et al., 2017).

    Formal Setup & Parameter Definitions

    For each demographic group \(a \in \{0, 1\}\): - Group base rate: \(p_a = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid A = a)\) with \(0 < p_a < 1\). - True Positive Rate: \(\text{TPR}_a = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = a) = 1 - \text{FNR}_a\). - False Positive Rate: \(\text{FPR}_a = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 0, A = a)\). - Positive Predictive Value: \(v_a = \text{PPV}_a = \mathbb{P}(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = a)\). - False Discovery Rate: \(\text{FDR}_a = 1 - v_a = \mathbb{P}(Y = 0 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = a)\).

    Step 1: Expansion of False Discovery Rate via Bayes' Theorem

    By definition of conditional probability: $\(\text{FDR}_a = \mathbb{P}(Y = 0 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = a) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 0, A = a) \mathbb{P}(Y = 0 \mid A = a)}{\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = a)}\)$

    Expanding the total probability in the denominator: $\(\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = a) = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 0, A = a)(1 - p_a) + \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = a) p_a = \text{FPR}_a (1 - p_a) + \text{TPR}_a p_a\)$

    Substituting back: $\(\text{FDR}_a = \frac{\text{FPR}_a (1 - p_a)}{\text{FPR}_a (1 - p_a) + \text{TPR}_a p_a}\)$

    Step 2: Isolating the False Positive Rate \(\text{FPR}_a\)

    Multiply both sides by the denominator: $\(\text{FDR}_a \left[ \text{FPR}_a (1 - p_a) + \text{TPR}_a p_a \right] = \text{FPR}_a (1 - p_a)\)$

    Distribute \(\text{FDR}_a\): $\(\text{FDR}_a \cdot \text{FPR}_a (1 - p_a) + \text{FDR}_a \cdot \text{TPR}_a p_a = \text{FPR}_a (1 - p_a)\)$

    Collect all terms containing \(\text{FPR}_a (1 - p_a)\) on the right-hand side: $\(\text{FDR}_a \cdot \text{TPR}_a p_a = (1 - \text{FDR}_a) \cdot \text{FPR}_a (1 - p_a)\)$

    Recognizing that \(1 - \text{FDR}_a = v_a = \text{PPV}_a\) and \(\text{TPR}_a = 1 - \text{FNR}_a\): $\((1 - v_a)(1 - \text{FNR}_a) p_a = v_a \cdot \text{FPR}_a (1 - p_a)\)$

    Dividing both sides by \(v_a (1 - p_a)\) yields the Chouldechova Fundamental Identity:

    \[\boxed{\text{FPR}_a = \left( \frac{p_a}{1 - p_a} \right) \cdot \left( \frac{1 - v_a}{v_a} \right) \cdot (1 - \text{FNR}_a)}\]

    Where \(\text{Odds}(p_a) = \frac{p_a}{1 - p_a}\) is the prior odds ratio of the positive ground-truth class in group \(a\).

    Step 3: Impossibility Ratio Under Imposed Equalized Constraints

    Suppose an algorithm simultaneously enforces: 1. Sufficiency (Predictive Parity): \(v_0 = v_1 = v\) (identical precision / PPV across groups). 2. Equality of Opportunity (Separation on Positives): \(\text{FNR}_0 = \text{FNR}_1 = \text{FNR}\) (identical false negative rates).

    Taking the direct algebraic ratio of the False Positive Rates across both groups:

    \[\frac{\text{FPR}_0}{\text{FPR}_1} = \frac{\left( \frac{p_0}{1 - p_0} \right) \left( \frac{1 - v}{v} \right) (1 - \text{FNR})}{\left( \frac{p_1}{1 - p_1} \right) \left( \frac{1 - v}{v} \right) (1 - \text{FNR})} = \frac{\text{Odds}(p_0)}{\text{Odds}(p_1)}\]
    \[\boxed{\frac{\text{FPR}_0}{\text{FPR}_1} = \frac{\frac{p_0}{1 - p_0}}{\frac{p_1}{1 - p_1}} = \frac{p_0 (1 - p_1)}{p_1 (1 - p_0)}}\]

    The Impossibility Theorem Statement

    Theorem (Kleinberg, Mullainathan, Raghavan 2017; Chouldechova 2017):
    Let base rates differ across groups (\(p_0 \ne p_1\)). A binary classifier \(\hat{Y}\) can simultaneously satisfy: 1. Equal Positive Predictive Value (\(\text{PPV}_0 = \text{PPV}_1\)) [Sufficiency] 2. Equal False Positive Rates (\(\text{FPR}_0 = \text{FPR}_1\)) [Separation: Error parity on negatives] 3. Equal False Negative Rates (\(\text{FNR}_0 = \text{FNR}_1\)) [Separation: Error parity on positives]

    if and only if the classifier achieves perfect deterministic classification (\(\text{FPR}_0 = \text{FPR}_1 = 0\) and \(\text{FNR}_0 = \text{FNR}_1 = 0\), implying \(v_0 = v_1 = 1\)).

    Concrete Real-World Consequence: The COMPAS Controversy

    In the Broward County COMPAS recidivism evaluation: - Group 0 (White defendants): \(p_0 \approx 0.35 \implies \text{Odds}(p_0) = \frac{0.35}{0.65} \approx 0.538\) - Group 1 (Black defendants): \(p_1 \approx 0.52 \implies \text{Odds}(p_1) = \frac{0.52}{0.48} \approx 1.083\) - Base Rate Odds Ratio: \(\frac{\text{Odds}(p_1)}{\text{Odds}(p_0)} = \frac{1.083}{0.538} \approx 2.01\)

    Northpointe engineered COMPAS to satisfy Calibration (\(\text{PPV}_0 \approx \text{PPV}_1 \approx 61\%\)). By the mathematical identity above, the Black defendant False Positive Rate was mathematically compelled to be exactly \(2.01\times\) higher than the White defendant False Positive Rate: $\(\text{FPR}_1 = 2.01 \times \text{FPR}_0 \implies 44.9\% \approx 2.01 \times 23.5\%\)$

    Neither Northpointe nor ProPublica made a mathematical error: - Northpointe defended Calibration (Sufficiency): "A score of 7 denotes equal risk." - ProPublica demonstrated Disparate Error Rates (Separation violation): "Innocent Black defendants are falsely flagged at double the rate." - The Kleinberg Theorem proved: Both claims were mathematically inevitable consequences of unequal arrest base rates.

    ⚖️ Individual Fairness: The Lipschitz Metric Continuity Guarantee

    Group fairness metrics aggregate populations, which can obscure severe mistreatment of specific individuals (a phenomenon termed Fairness Gerrymandering). In 2012, Cynthia Dwork, Moritz Hardt, Toniann Pitassi, Omer Reingold, and Richard Zemel introduced Individual Fairness grounded in Aristotle's principle of justice: "Treat similar cases similarly, and different cases differently in proportion to their difference."

    Formal Mathematical Definition

    Let \(\mathcal{X}\) denote the feature space of individuals equipped with a metric \(d_{\mathcal{X}}: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\) measuring task-relevant similarity. Let \(\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\) denote the space of probability distributions over outcomes \(\mathcal{Y}\), equipped with a statistical distance metric \(D_{\mathcal{Y}}\) (e.g., total variation or Wasserstein distance).

    A mapping \(M: \mathcal{X} \to \Delta(\mathcal{Y})\) satisfies \((D_{\mathcal{Y}}, d_{\mathcal{X}})\)-Lipschitz Individual Fairness with constant \(L > 0\) if:

    \[\boxed{D_{\mathcal{Y}}(M(x_1), M(x_2)) \le L \cdot d_{\mathcal{X}}(x_1, x_2), \quad \forall x_1, x_2 \in \mathcal{X}}\]

    For scalar score functions \(f: \mathcal{X} \to [0, 1]\), the condition requires: $\(|f(x_1) - f(x_2)| \le L \cdot d_{\mathcal{X}}(x_1, x_2)\)$

    The Fundamental Obstacle: Metric Specification

    While mathematically rigorous, individual fairness shifts the entire normative problem onto the definition of the metric \(d_{\mathcal{X}}\): 1. Normative Subjectivity: Who decides whether a credit applicant with 10 years of steady employment but no college degree is "closer" to a recent Ivy League graduate with 6 months of employment? 2. Proxy Contamination: Standard Euclidean or Mahalanobis distances over observed features reintroduce historical bias because features (e.g., ZIP code, arrest history, credit utilization) correlate strongly with protected attributes.

    🔮 Counterfactual Fairness via Structural Causal Models

    Correlation-based fairness metrics fail when sensitive attributes causally distort the features themselves. In 2017, Kusner, Loftus, Russell, and Silva formulated Counterfactual Fairness using Judea Pearl's Structural Causal Models (SCMs) (Kusner et al., 2017).

    Formal SCM Definition

    A Structural Causal Model consists of: - Sensitive attribute \(A \in \mathcal{A}\) - Observable features \(X \in \mathcal{X}\) - Latent exogenous variables \(U \in \mathcal{U}\) representing unobserved background factors independent of \(A\) (\(U \perp A\)) - Structural assignment equations: \(X = g(A, U)\) and \(Y = h(X, A, U)\)

    The Counterfactual Fairness Criterion

    A predictor \(\hat{Y} = f(X, A)\) is Counterfactually Fair if, for every individual with observed features \(X = x\) and protected attribute \(A = a\):

    \[\boxed{\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y}_{A \leftarrow a}(U) = y \mid X = x, A = a) = \mathbb{P}(\hat{Y}_{A \leftarrow a'}(U) = y \mid X = x, A = a), \quad \forall a, a' \in \mathcal{A}, \forall y \in \mathcal{Y}}\]

    Interventional Interpretation

    The prediction for a specific individual must remain invariant in a counterfactual parallel world where their sensitive attribute \(A\) was altered (via do-calculus intervention \(do(A = a')\)), while all non-descendant background traits \(U\) remained identical.

    Theorem: Sufficient Condition for Counterfactual Fairness

    A predictor \(f\) is counterfactually fair if it is a function solely of non-descendants of \(A\) in the causal DAG, or a function solely of the inferred exogenous background variables \(U\): $\(\hat{Y} = f(U)\)$

    Any model that trains directly on features \(X\) that are causal descendants of \(A\) (e.g., GPA, salary, insurance claims) will violate counterfactual fairness unless explicitly adjusted for the direct and mediated paths from \(A\).

    📈 The Fairness-Accuracy Pareto Frontier

    Enforcing any fairness constraint constitutes a restriction on the unconstrained Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) hypothesis space. Let \(\mathcal{H}\) be the unrestricted hypothesis class and \(\mathcal{H}_{\text{fair}} \subset \mathcal{H}\) the subset satisfying fairness constraint \(\mathcal{D}(h) \le \epsilon\):

    \[\mathcal{R}_{\text{fair}}^* = \min_{h \in \mathcal{H}_{\text{fair}}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{emp}}(h) \ge \min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{emp}}(h) = \mathcal{R}_{\text{unconstrained}}^*\]

    The difference \(\mathcal{R}_{\text{fair}}^* - \mathcal{R}_{\text{unconstrained}}^*\) represents the Cost of Fairness (Corbett-Davies et al., 2017; Berk et al., 2021).

def audit_chouldechova_impossibility(
    p0: float, p1: float, ppv: float, fnr: float, atol: float = 1e-4
) -> ChouldechovaAuditContract:
    """
    Algebraically audits Chouldechova's fundamental identity:
    FPR_a = [p_a / (1 - p_a)] * [(1 - v_a) / v_a] * (1 - FNR_a)
    """
    odds_0 = p0 / (1.0 - p0)
    odds_1 = p1 / (1.0 - p1)
    ppv_factor = (1.0 - ppv) / ppv
    fnr_factor = 1.0 - fnr

    fpr_0 = odds_0 * ppv_factor * fnr_factor
    fpr_1 = odds_1 * ppv_factor * fnr_factor

    fpr_ratio = fpr_0 / fpr_1 if fpr_1 > 0 else 0.0
    odds_ratio = odds_0 / odds_1 if odds_1 > 0 else 0.0

    identity_verified = bool(np.isclose(fpr_ratio, odds_ratio, atol=atol))
    impossibility_active = bool(not np.isclose(fpr_ratio, 1.0, atol=atol))

    return ChouldechovaAuditContract(
        group_0_base_rate=round(p0, 4),
        group_1_base_rate=round(p1, 4),
        calibrated_ppv=round(ppv, 4),
        target_fnr=round(fnr, 4),
        group_0_forced_fpr=round(fpr_0, 4),
        group_1_forced_fpr=round(fpr_1, 4),
        fpr_ratio=round(fpr_ratio, 4),
        odds_ratio=round(odds_ratio, 4),
        identity_verified=identity_verified,
        impossibility_active=impossibility_active,
    )


def audit_lipschitz_fairness(
    features: np.ndarray,
    scores: np.ndarray,
    lipschitz_threshold: float = 0.50
) -> LipschitzIndividualFairnessContract:
    """
    Audits individual fairness via pairwise Lipschitz quotient:
    L_ij = |f(x_i) - f(x_j)| / ||x_i - x_j||_2
    """
    n = features.shape[0]
    diffs = features[:, None, :] - features[None, :, :]
    dists = np.linalg.norm(diffs, axis=-1)
    np.fill_diagonal(dists, np.inf)

    score_diffs = np.abs(scores[:, None] - scores[None, :])
    quotients = score_diffs / dists
    valid_quotients = quotients[np.isfinite(quotients)]

    max_l = float(np.max(valid_quotients))
    p95_l = float(np.percentile(valid_quotients, 95))

    return LipschitzIndividualFairnessContract(
        n_individuals=n,
        feature_dimension=features.shape[1],
        empirical_max_lipschitz=round(max_l, 4),
        p95_lipschitz=round(p95_l, 4),
        lipschitz_threshold=lipschitz_threshold,
        satisfies_individual_fairness=bool(max_l <= lipschitz_threshold),
    )


def audit_counterfactual_fairness(
    observed_x: np.ndarray,
    observed_a: int,
    causal_beta: np.ndarray,
    classifier_weights: np.ndarray,
    use_latent_invariant: bool = False,
    atol: float = 1e-4
) -> CounterfactualAuditContract:
    """
    Audits Counterfactual Fairness under SCM:
    X = beta * A + U  =>  U = X - beta * A
    Under intervention A <- 1 - A:
    X_cf = beta * (1 - A) + U
    """
    # Infer latent exogenous background U
    u_inferred = observed_x - causal_beta * observed_a

    # Counterfactual intervention
    a_counterfactual = 1 - observed_a
    x_counterfactual = causal_beta * a_counterfactual + u_inferred

    if use_latent_invariant:
        # Counterfactually fair predictor operates solely on U
        w_u = classifier_weights
        score_factual = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-float(np.dot(w_u, u_inferred))))
        score_cf = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-float(np.dot(w_u, u_inferred))))
    else:
        # Standard biased predictor operates on mediated X
        score_factual = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-float(np.dot(classifier_weights, observed_x))))
        score_cf = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-float(np.dot(classifier_weights, x_counterfactual))))

    disparity = abs(score_factual - score_cf)

    return CounterfactualAuditContract(
        observed_sensitive_attribute=observed_a,
        observed_score=round(score_factual, 4),
        counterfactual_score=round(score_cf, 4),
        counterfactual_disparity=round(disparity, 4),
        is_counterfactually_fair=bool(np.isclose(disparity, 0.0, atol=atol)),
    )

    Explain how an algorithmic credit model could achieve perfect Demographic Parity across race (Black vs. White) and perfect Demographic Parity across gender (Male vs. Female), yet engage in severe discrimination against Black female applicants.

    Reveal Detailed Solution

    This failure mode is termed Fairness Gerrymandering (Kearns et al., 2018): - Suppose the model approves \(50\%\) of Black applicants and \(50\%\) of White applicants (Demographic Parity on Race = \(1.0\)). - Suppose the model approves \(50\%\) of Male applicants and \(50\%\) of Female applicants (Demographic Parity on Gender = \(1.0\)). - However, within the Black demographic, the model approves \(100\%\) of Black Men (\(N=50\)) and \(0\%\) of Black Women (\(N=50\)). - Within the White demographic, the model approves \(0\%\) of White Men (\(N=50\)) and \(100\%\) of White Women (\(N=50\)). - Both marginal group fairness constraints are perfectly satisfied: - \(\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y}=1 \mid \text{Black}) = 50/100 = 50\%\), \(\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y}=1 \mid \text{White}) = 50/100 = 50\%\) - \(\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y}=1 \mid \text{Male}) = 50/100 = 50\%\), \(\mathbb{P}(\hat{Y}=1 \mid \text{Female}) = 50/100 = 50\%\) - Yet Black women suffer a \(0\%\) approval rate. Subgroup and intersectional auditing, as well as Lipschitz individual fairness bounds, prevent marginal parity from concealing structured subgroup exclusion.

    Check 4: Kusner's Counterfactual Invariance vs. Fairness Through Unawareness

    A bank trains a loan approval model without including the applicant's protected attribute \(A\) (race), satisfying "Fairness Through Unawareness." However, the model includes zip code (\(Z\)) and university attended (\(U\)), both of which are descendant mediators of \(A\) in the causal DAG (\(A \to Z\) and \(A \to U\)).

    Explain why this model violates Kusner's Counterfactual Fairness definition, and state the exact Pearlian interventional condition that must be satisfied.

    Reveal Detailed Solution & Proof

    1. Why Fairness Through Unawareness Fails: Simply omitting \(A\) from the feature matrix \(\mathcal{X}\) does not eliminate the causal influence of \(A\). Because \(Z\) and \(U\) are causally downstream of \(A\) (\(A \to Z \to \hat{Y}\) and \(A \to U \to \hat{Y}\)), the model reconstructs \(A\) through proxy encoding.

    2. Kusner's Counterfactual Fairness Condition: A predictor \(\hat{Y} = f(\mathcal{X})\) is counterfactually fair if and only if for all individuals with observed evidence \(\mathcal{X} = x, A = a\): $\(\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{Y}_{A \leftarrow a}(\mathcal{U}) = 1 \;\middle|\; \mathcal{X} = x, A = a\right) = \mathbb{P}\left(\hat{Y}_{A \leftarrow a'}(\mathcal{U}) = 1 \;\middle|\; \mathcal{X} = x, A = a\right), \quad \forall a, a' \in \mathcal{A}\)$ where \(\mathcal{U}\) represents the unobserved exogenous background factors.

    3. Causal Mediation Proof: When we intervene \(\text{do}(A \leftarrow a')\), the interventional values of the mediators change: $\(Z_{A \leftarrow a'} \ne Z_{A \leftarrow a}, \qquad U_{A \leftarrow a'} \ne U_{A \leftarrow a}\)$ Since \(\hat{Y}\) depends on \(Z\) and \(U\), \(\hat{Y}_{A \leftarrow a'} \ne \hat{Y}_{A \leftarrow a}\), resulting in a non-zero counterfactual disparity \(\Delta_{\text{CF}} > 0\). To achieve counterfactual fairness, the model must predict using only the abducted exogenous variables \(\mathcal{U}\) or non-descendant features of \(A\).
